title: Elsa and Madeline

This is a tale about Elsa and Madeline - a story of inspiration, and attunement, and beholding: of people, ideas, and experiences.

I have not spoken to either of them in a long time, so I write this from memory, and memory, as I have seen often, colours things. I have tried to be honest in the telling.

Elsa and Madeline were good friends first, though Madeline was practically Elsa's wife. Anyone close to Elsa learned quickly how much she cared about Madeline, and how much she loved her company.

I came to know Madeline through Elsa. Elsa was my best friend, and Madeline reached me first as an epicentre in a lot of Elsa's stories and only later as a person. One of my first real interactions with her was a threat. If I ever hurt Elsa, Madeline would burn off all my beautiful, bouncy hair. We have come a long way since.

It took me time to know Madeline directly at all. For a long while, Elsa was my bridge to her. Elsa spoke about Madeline with such simplicity and authenticity that I felt I knew her better than Madeline felt known by me. Without meaning to, I began to take Madeline's presence in my life for granted, and as a very close friend, when really it was Elsa who was close to her, and Elsa who had built the bridge between us. So I was a spectator of Madeline's life, largely through Elsa's eyes, partly through my own. Still, the picture I held was reasonably accurate, because Elsa knew her very well and was faithful to her in the telling. And somewhere across three-ish years, Madeline and I came a long way of our own. We became friends. I understood her a little, I think. I felt understood by her a lot.

That feeling of being understood was not an accident of chemistry. What the two of them had, and what I was slow to name, was that they saw each other for who they truly were, and they met the world with an experiential curiosity. They were deeply empathetic, empathetic by instinct, not through anecdotes or shallow tricks picked up from a book. It was hard to recognise this during all those evenings, because its effect was so plain. I simply felt seen and heard for being myself, without having to act any differently.

Their judgement, too, was remarkable, especially about people and experiences. As Elsa's best friend I entrusted her with many of my decisions, and she was rarely wrong. Madeline was an empath and a person of rare agency; she felt more, and understood people more, sometimes more than they understood themselves. I realised only much later how sharp her judgement was. Time and again I would begin with "Hey, I need your help with this," or "I need your opinion on this," without thinking much about it.

That trust had been built early. When I was adjusting to a new city I leaned on Elsa a lot, and Madeline became part of that too. I am grateful to have spent time with them both; they brought warmth and homeliness into an otherwise cold world.

In time the two of them went from being friends to being roommates. Their first house together was on a second floor, at an address that read like a poem about the neighbourhood - a building in pink, with gates in white. Inside, a long hall ran from the front door to the far end, and two cosy rooms opened off it. A beautiful black couch set sat in the middle of the hall, and therefore in the middle of the house. At the far end, they built a lovely, well-stocked kitchen.

Every morning a cool breeze moved down the hall and through the whole house, and the sunlight came in at angles that lit it best. Waking up there was always a pleasant experience, and a forgiving one.

It was a home, not just a house. Friends and family wandered through it between jobs, cities and people, or simply between stretches of life, and the house held them all. Each of the two attended to it in her own way. Madeline took on project after project and was forever onto something that would make the place prettier; Elsa made it homely. Their house was filled with travel charms from all over the world. I spent countless hours there and understood these qualities only later, when I failed to turn my house into a home.

Being there so often also meant I was occupying a lot of their space. I was stepping over their boundaries, and I took some of that - no, a lot of it - for granted.

Yet neither of them ever let me feel any friction. It was all free-flowing, so natural, and that ease was entirely the doing of these two women; I came away with a deep, deep admiration for them both.

They had a douchebag jar for the house, and I have lost count of how much I owe them.

And I watched how they were with each other. They greeted each other with spontaneity. They had many adventures together - I hope they still do - and I was happy to hear their stories, to support them through those adventures, or to join them. They knew when and how to ask for help, without making it hard for each other.

I witnessed their conflicts, too, and how they debated them out. I, then a conflict-averse people pleaser, would try to step in and dissolve the tension. But the maturity of their disagreements, the respect with which they held them, and their ability to repair the relationship afterwards were beautiful to watch. It was graceful.

They showed me what genuine care and love look like, and what it means to have each other's back. There was a day when Madeline was having a rough time, and Elsa spent hours making her comfort dish despite not being great at it (I helped, of course). It was only later, in the absence of such love, that I understood what love without strings feels like - care, affection, and the wish to make the other person's life a little better, a little happier.

I was not always a good student of it. When we were all new to the city, Elsa kept me a great deal of company, and she was constantly trying to balance her time between me and Madeline. I, like an entitled brat, threw tantrums. I had not really spent time alone back then. Now, after countless hours of my own, I can see that on some days Madeline spent her evenings entirely by herself - and was still enough, still a force of nature through it all.

Eventually Elsa and I grew distant. Life happened, and perhaps that distance was what each needed to find themselves. One consequence was that I lost touch with Madeline as well.

I think about them both a lot. Actually, not that much - just when the water is boiling, while I'm making coffee.

I continue to be a cheerleader for them, of course - from the sidelines. They say you can't change anyone; you can only inspire them. I, for one, have certainly been inspired by Elsa and Madeline.
