## I. Running

We don't teach toddlers to walk the way we teach them to read.

Through trial and error, and by watching others, a child learns to balance, coordinate their legs, and move. Soon they discover that a certain push and pull lets them move faster, and that is running.

Walking and running are self-correcting. They tell you immediately when you're doing something wrong. You stumble, you adjust, you improve, all on instinct.

Now compare a casual jog to a marathon.

Jogging isn't rocket science. Anyone can learn it, and trial and error carries you most of the way. A marathon is different. It demands training, coaching, and continuous re-learning. Nutrition, clothing, pacing, form — each reveals its effect long after the decision is made.

The duration for the feedback loop determines how quickly you can self learn.

Jogging has a short loop. Effort and consequence sit close together. The marathon has a long one. If your form is wrong you won't feel it at kilometre two — it shows up somewhere in the middle of the race, or in how you finish. Treat the marathon as a skill you learn once and simply practise, and your form drifts, because nothing is there to catch the error in time.

So the first question to ask about any activity is: how quickly does it give you the feedback?

## II. Pourovers

I brew a cup of coffee every morning. Some days it's excellent and I'm proud of myself. Other days I'm drinking straw water.

Every few weeks I'd end up with 4-5 bad brews. The fix for them was to go back to my notes and follow the written steps instead of the remembered ones.

A cup is the sum of a dozen small decisions: grind size, dose, water temperature, pour rate, how long you let it bloom. When it tastes bad, you can identify almost immediately. It took me a lot of time to learn how to identify what is leading me to here.

Part of that was not having the words. The rest was that a bad cup tells you something is wrong without telling you which of the twelve things went wrong.

So a feedback loop can fail in two ways. It can be slow, and you find out too late. Or it can be ambiguous, and you find out immediately but can't trace back to the input.

## III. Understood Backward

Delay and ambiguity are properties of the activity. What you bring to them is a memory that rewrites the past while you wait.

By the time the result arrives, you've forgotten the decisions that produced it, or maybe, replaced them with a simpler story. 

This is why my notes helped in course correction. They were a record of what I actually did, not what I thought I did. 

A log alone isn't enough. It captures; it doesn't interpret. It has to be paired with a review ritual. Sitting with the record and reading it against the outcome. Together they help to separate the signal from noise.

Runners keep training logs. Scientists keep lab notebooks. Investors keep journals. Coffee nerds track their brews. All of them are solving the same problem. Reconstructing causality after the fact. 

When feedback arrives late, or arrives without explanation, improvement depends less on experience than on your ability to remember what the experience actually was.
