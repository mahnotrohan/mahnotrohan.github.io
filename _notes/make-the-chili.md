> A friend of mine unexpectedly lost his wife. A couple of months later we were golfing, talking about nothing in particular. He asked what my dinner plans were. I told him my wife wanted to make homemade chili and cornbread, but I didn't feel like stopping at the store.
> 
> 
> He said quietly, "Make the chili."
> 

I have wanted to write this piece for a long time. It has been marinating in my head. I was never sure what to call this. I love reading memoirs, yet writing one has always felt like declaring that the important chapters are already finished, that no more adventures lie ahead. That is probably why I never wrote an "As We Leave", the farewell essay seniors at IIT Kanpur write before graduating, even though I read countless others.

The last eighteen months have been nothing short of a character-defining arc. Who I am today is very different from the person I imagined one, two, or five years ago. This is an attempt to piece myself together.

At the start of every year I make a list of things I want to pursue. Every item on this year's list has led me to this point. As Goldsmith put it - "What got you here won't get you there." 
And before I move on, I want to look back, connect the dots, and make sense of what happened.

In February 2025 I was set to join a new job. I was excited and nervous at once. It was a dream come true. I knew about the company through friends, but I had no clear sense of what the role would actually demand. I was also moving house.

Telling my then-flatmates was not easy. Not being able to talk about it fully made it worse. I disliked announcing the decision instead of discussing it, listening to them, and showing them how much they mattered to me. We had all changed since college. Having people around who knew me made life a little better.

After I moved out, I thought about calling or visiting them almost every weekend. That’s fifty times in a year. Almost every time, discomfort won. I did not make the chili, I guess.

Picking up a whole life and adjusting to a new place and a new job was hectic. I tried to make it all feel natural. I mistook busyness for effectiveness.

Life arrived with full force, and I never paused to ask for help, to talk about what I was feeling, or simply just exist. I remember one weekend when I was genuinely excited because I finally had time with my then-partner and nothing occupying my headspace.

Then came a stretch when I was completely alone. I had never been this alone in Bangalore or, perhaps, in my life. For seven odd days I was living in an empty house. None of my roommates were there. No friends or family I could call over or crash with. Work had just started getting difficult. It was an upsetting experience.

I discovered the echoes of silence. They rattled around my head and fed the anxiety. I spent twelve hours or more at work to avoid being alone in the house. When I was home I filled the background with any voice I could find. The loneliness I had been running from caught up with me. It was hard. I made it through, it was hard. I was not kind to myself. I kept pushing when what I needed was some company, and some comfort.

What followed over the next couple of months felt like standing in a hailstorm. I became a more anxious person than I had ever been, holding on to people and things so tightly that the holding hurt more than losing them would have. It is strange what seven days of absolute silence can do to the brain that years of ordinary life could not. I wonder, is this why people go to Vipassana?

My partner and I split up after a long relationship. It became a slow lesson in love and grief. You can grieve not being loved by someone. You do not grieve the fact that you loved them. Life is generous enough to let you pour that intensity elsewhere. I grieved the life we had built together. It had been marvellous.

I had uprooted my entire life and was trying to re-centre it when I was asked to rebuild it.

I chose running and work as my two crutches for support. I spent long hours at the office. There was a great deal to learn, a great deal to do, and many things to fail at. Outside work I continued training for a half marathon I had signed up for. I ran almost every other day.

Most of the people I met at work were smart, kind, and fun to be around. Still, there were days that steadily wore me down. My confidence was already fragile; certain interactions left me feeling smaller and smaller until I felt like nothing. Zero, shunya.

Every morning, grappling with my muted-ness and the anxiety, I went for a run. I pushed my heart rate to 200 bpm on the rocky trails around the lakes, pulled myself together, packed lunch, and went to the office.

Those hours were rough. I lost count of the times I took Combiflam because I could not stop shaking. I did not handle it well at the time. Experience still gives you wisdom, no matter how it arrives. Since then I have faced bigger and more complex problems - not only for myself, but while trying to be present for others. It took time and many runs to get better.

That is what running meant to me. No matter who I was, where I stood, or what I had, it rewarded showing up. It is a Sisyphean task, one I felt privileged to repeat so many times.

I had trained, just not well. With nothing more than trust in myself that I could finish, I ran 21 kilometres through the streets of Bangalore. Twenty-one kilometres is a lot of kilometres. You can think about everything under the sun and still have a lot many kilometres smiling at you. If you have nothing to look forward to, covering that distance gets challenging.

I was running with a tinge of emptiness - shunya - inside me. Every day until then had been spent training for this single day.

The stretch between 15 and 17 kilometres was the toughest. Covered in salt and sweat, hands sticky with caramel gel (a bad flavour choice), tears rolling down my face, I finished. I did it. And then the emptiness hit me like a giant truck. Some of it was runner's blues. The heavier blow was the deep emptiness, the silence and darkness that settled in as September 2025 ended.

The next six months were a cocktail I had not planned to order. October and November were the big sad. I was awake at ungodly hours, drinking copious amounts of coffee and reading voraciously. If December had not arrived I might have simply melted into the grey couch.

December was different though. Each week was a new place, and brought a different set of people, with fun adventures. I ran another half marathon, this time in Goa. It was a breathtaking experience. I returned to work with more zeal for life, ready for whatever curveballs came next.

Then I had a small incident that injured my knees. I thought I would rest for a while. Instead of running I was glued to the bed and the couch. I found solace in reading and in watching cuckoo shows. I assumed, as always, that I would heal.

Despite the long list of books I had finished, it felt that need to pay another visit to the doctor. What I dreaded had happened - I was told I had torn a ligament that would require surgery. I grieved for a long time. After the operation things would never be quite the same. My plans of running beautiful trails were crushed. Countless nights were spent processing it. It was a lot.

Naturally, to handle the overwhelm, I was PM-ing my life. A great deal had changed over the previous twelve to fifteen months, and so had I. I had lost many relationships, both explicitly and implicitly. Some people I could no longer meet where they were; some could no longer meet me where I was.

I am grateful that I made it through it all, and that I could find support when I needed it.

I am finishing this essay on a flight to Bangalore, post my ACL surgery, returning after two months. I am recovering well. There is a long road ahead, but I am happy simply to be wearing jeans again. I took four weeks off work for the operation and early recovery - the first time I had done so at this job. Those four weeks were mostly good, except for the night I came home from the hospital: a night of nightmares and a brutal test of pain tolerance. In the past seven weeks I have read a great deal of fiction, drunk a lot of espresso, babysat, and most of all learned to walk again.

Right now I feel clueless. Everything I had planned in life only went up to this point. There are no open items on the old to-do list I can pick up.

That emptiness leaves a broader horizon: space for unplanned experiences, for dreaming bigger, for pursuing the things I have always wanted but never worked towards. I had planned to go to Avartana the day before this year's trajectory changed. I still want to run the Wipro and Ladakh marathons when I am able. Someday, do a triathlon too.

I am doing okay in life, where I am. I'm getting through the days. I measure life in weeks, and luckily, there hasn't been a dull week - at least the plot is tracking good!

I have become more accepting of everything life shows up with. I am living closer to the way I once hoped to live. I am almost the person I always wished to become. The Rohan of 2022 would be surprised and proud; most of what I am and what I have experienced would have been inconceivable to him.

Some of my close ones have also had their own seven days alone. I empathise with them. I've tried to show up or stay back to ensure that they have company. It is uncomfortable to be this alone, and hard to voice it.

I am grateful to every person who has been part of this story.

And so I keep making the chili.
