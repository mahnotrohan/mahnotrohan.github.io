A derivation, not a survey

The machinery of power

Every political system — a republic, a junta, a board of directors, a housing society, a Slack channel with moderators, a family at dinner — is solving the same problem with different hardware. This builds the problem up from nothing, then shows you the machines people have built to solve it, then shows you the loops that make those machines drift, ossify and collapse.

The one-sentence version. Politics is what happens when people who want different things cannot cheaply get away from each other. Everything else — constitutions, coups, coalitions, promotion committees — is engineering downstream of that.
01 — Foundations

Why politics exists at all

Don't start from governments. Start from the conditions that make governing necessary. There are four, and they are load-bearing: knock any one out and politics dissolves.

The definition this gives you

A political system is any arrangement that produces decisions binding on members who disagree, and that survives the disagreement.

Notice what is not in that definition: no states, no elections, no ideology, no scale. That is deliberate. The definition is substrate-independent, which is why the same analysis will run on a nation of 1.4 billion and on a five-person team, and why the failure modes rhyme across both.

The move that does all the work Two variables decide how elaborate a political system has to be: the cost of exit and the stakes of the decision. Cheap exit and low stakes produce almost no politics — people simply leave. Expensive exit and high stakes produce constitutions, courts, armies and centuries of argument. Everything in this page is a function of those two numbers.

The primitives

Six terms, defined tightly, do most of the explanatory work from here on. Get these exact and the rest assembles itself.

Power
The capacity to change what someone else does. Comes in three flavours: coercion (do it or suffer), exchange (do it and be paid), authority (do it because it's the rule). Only the third is cheap at scale.
Authority
Power that is recognised as rightful, so it does not have to be re-imposed each time. The whole game is converting coercion into authority, because coercion is metered and authority is flat-rate.
Legitimacy
The stock of belief that makes authority hold. Max Weber's three sources: tradition (it has always been so), charisma (this person is extraordinary), legal-rational (the procedure was followed). Modern systems mostly run on the third and quietly top up with the other two.
Institution
A rule that persists beyond the people currently inside it. Institutions are memory: they let a group keep a commitment its present members would rather break.
The state
Weber again: the organisation that successfully claims a monopoly on legitimate violence within a territory. "Successfully" and "legitimate" are the load-bearing words — where either fails, you get warlords, mafias or militias, which are not the absence of political order but a competing supplier of it.
Sovereignty
The claim that no higher authority can overrule you. It is a claim, not a fact; it holds exactly as long as others behave as though it does.
02 — Foundations

The five functions every system must perform

Whatever the constitution says, a political system is a machine with five jobs. If you want to understand an unfamiliar system quickly, find where each job is done and who does it. Everything else is detail.

Aggregation — turn many preferences into one decision

Voting, hierarchy, markets, consensus, lottery, seniority, or a fight. There is no neutral method; the method changes the answer.

Execution — actually do the thing

Administration, bureaucracy, delegated agents. The moment you delegate, you have a principal–agent problem, and it never goes away.

Enforcement — make the decision bind the unwilling

Police, courts, sanctions, fines, exclusion, shunning, a bad performance rating. Note that enforcement capacity is always a tiny fraction of the population.

Revision — change the decision, and the deciders, without killing the group

Elections, amendment, succession, impeachment, reorg, fork, coup, revolution. This is the function most systems get wrong, and the one that kills them.

Legitimation — make compliance mostly voluntary

The story that explains why the other four are rightful. Not decoration: it is the cost-reduction layer that makes the whole machine affordable.

The enforcement arithmetic Police forces run on the order of a few hundred officers per 100,000 people. If even a small fraction of a population decides simultaneously to stop complying, enforcement cannot physically cope. This is the single most important structural fact about political order, and it is why every regime, including the most brutal, spends enormous effort on being believed in.
03 — Foundations

Three kinds of problem, three different solutions

People lump all group difficulties together. They are not the same shape, and confusing them is the most common analytic error in both politics and management. Diagnose the shape first; the right instrument follows.

Type 1

Coordination

Everyone is fine with any rule, but there must be a rule. Which side of the road. Which time zone. Which file format.

Solved by: convention, a focal point, or anyone credible simply announcing it. Self-enforcing once set — nobody gains by defecting alone.

Cost: near zero. If your problem is really this, stop building governance.

Type 2

Collective action

Everyone benefits if it's done, nobody individually gains by doing it. Defence, clean air, roads, code review, writing the runbook.

Solved by: coercion (taxes), selective incentives (perks for contributors), or small-group mutual monitoring. Mancur Olson's result: large groups under-organise, small ones over-organise.

Cost: real, ongoing enforcement.

Type 3

Distribution

Who gets what. Roughly zero-sum. Land, budget, headcount, status, the corner office, the disputed province.

Solved by: nothing cleanly. Only power, procedure, or a legitimacy story that makes the losers accept the loss. More information does not help — often it sharpens the fight.

Cost: this is where political systems earn their keep.

Diagnostic Most arguments that feel unsolvable are Type 3 problems being addressed with Type 1 tools. If someone responds to a budget fight by circulating more data, they have misdiagnosed the shape. Data settles coordination; it does not settle distribution.

Why the three are hard in different ways

Olson's free-rider logic explains why the beneficiaries of broad goods — consumers, taxpayers, the general public — organise badly, while narrow interests organise brilliantly. A policy that costs 300 million people one rupee each and gives 300 people a crore each will be lobbied for ferociously and opposed by nobody. Concentrated benefits and diffuse costs is the single most reliable predictor of bad policy in any system, democratic or not, national or corporate.

Elinor Ostrom's contribution was to show that Type 2 is not a binary between "privatise it" and "have the state run it". Communities have governed shared forests, fisheries and irrigation for centuries using a third path — and she extracted the conditions under which it works. They are listed in §07, and they transfer directly to shared engineering platforms.

04 — Foundations

The hard limits: what aggregation cannot do

This is the part most political education skips, and the part a systems thinker most needs. There are proved impossibility results about turning many preferences into one choice. They are not practical difficulties to be engineered away. They are theorems.

Arrow's impossibility theorem 1951

No ranked voting rule over three or more options can simultaneously satisfy unanimity, independence of irrelevant alternatives, transitivity, and non-dictatorship. Something always has to give.

What it means: there is no uniquely correct way to add up what a group wants. "The will of the people" is not a well-defined object waiting to be measured.

Gibbard–Satterthwaite 1973–75

Any non-dictatorial deterministic rule choosing among three or more outcomes can be manipulated — some voter can sometimes do better by misrepresenting their preference.

What it means: strategic voting is not corruption of the system, it is a structural feature of it. Designing it out is impossible; you can only choose which manipulations you prefer.

McKelvey's chaos theorem 1976

When choices sit in two or more dimensions, majority rule has no stable winner: a clever agenda-setter can walk the group, one majority-approved step at a time, to almost any outcome.

What it means: whoever controls the agenda controls the result. The order of the questions matters as much as the preferences. This is the single most useful thing on this page.

The median voter result Black, Downs

In one dimension with single-peaked preferences, the median voter's choice wins. Two competitors converge toward the middle.

What it means: the pull to the centre in two-party systems is structural, not cowardice — and it evaporates the moment politics becomes genuinely multidimensional, which is when chaos returns.

The consequence to carry forward Because no aggregation rule is neutral, procedure is substance. Choosing first-past-the-post over proportional representation, or deciding whether the committee votes on amendments before or after the main motion, is not administrative housekeeping — it selects outcomes. Anyone who tells you a process choice is "just process" is either naive or winning.

Two more structural facts

The principal–agent problem. The moment you delegate, your agent has their own interests and better information than you about what they actually did. Every institution — audits, elections, term limits, reporting lines, boards, inspectors general — is a partial and expensive patch on this one hole. It is never closed, only narrowed.

Exit, voice and loyalty. Albert Hirschman: when something you belong to deteriorates, you have three responses — leave, complain, or stay quiet. The crucial insight is that these trade off. Where exit is cheap, voice institutions stay thin, because the dissatisfied leave rather than organise. Where exit is expensive, voice machinery becomes elaborate. This alone explains why nation-states have constitutions and companies have exit interviews.

06 — The map

Atlas of regimes

Now the familiar names — but read each as a region in the space above, not a box. The badge gives the typical coalition width; the fields give the loop that dominates and the way this design usually dies. Roughly a third of the world's states are electoral or competitive authoritarian rather than cleanly democratic or cleanly autocratic; the hybrids are the norm, not the exception.

Liberal democracy

W: wide

Competitive elections plus rights that bind the majority itself. The second half is what makes it liberal; elections alone do not.

Dominant loop
Accountability — poor performance removes leaders, which corrects policy.
Characteristic failure
Capture by organised interests; polarisation eroding the guardrails; short-termism from cycle/payoff mismatch.
Key variants
Parliamentary vs presidential; majoritarian (Westminster) vs consensus/proportional (Lijphart's consociational model for divided societies).

Electoral / competitive authoritarian

W: narrow

Elections are real and contested, but the field is tilted: state media, selective prosecution, funding asymmetry, tame courts. The modal non-democracy of the present era.

Dominant loop
Tilt reinforces incumbency, which funds more tilt.
Characteristic failure
Either tips to full autocracy, or loses an election it miscalculated and unravels fast.
Tell
Opposition is allowed to run but not allowed to win. Levitsky and Way's diagnostic.

Dominant-party state

W: narrow

One party monopolises power, but real institutions exist inside it — factions, promotion ladders, term norms, internal bargaining. Politics moves from between parties to within one.

Dominant loop
Party performance legitimacy: deliver growth, retain mandate, fund more delivery.
Characteristic failure
Performance legitimacy is a treadmill — a downturn attacks the only story available. Succession is the perennial danger.
Strength
Can plan on horizons elected governments cannot, and can pivot fast.

Personalist autocracy

W: tiny

Power attaches to an individual rather than an office. Institutions persist on paper but are hollowed; loyalty outranks competence in every appointment.

Dominant loop
Information trap plus repression trap, both running simultaneously.
Characteristic failure
Highest outcome variance of any regime type — and a guaranteed succession crisis, because the system is the person.
Tell
No one around the leader can deliver bad news and keep their job.

Military regime / junta

W: tiny

The enforcement arm becomes the decider. Usually justified as a temporary corrective to disorder.

Dominant loop
Coup-proofing: the ruler must now defend against the institution that made him.
Characteristic failure
Shortest average lifespan of the autocracies — internal splits, since the people who can remove you are in the room.

Monarchy

W: tiny to narrow

Hereditary succession, traditional legitimation. Absolute monarchies rule; constitutional ones reign while others rule, converting the crown into a legitimacy reservoir rather than a decision-maker.

Dominant loop
Tradition compounds: the longer it has lasted, the more rightful it seems.
Characteristic failure
Competence lottery; and legitimacy that cannot be rebuilt once broken, because its whole basis was continuity.

Theocracy

W: narrow

Legitimation derived from divine authority, with clerical bodies holding veto or interpretive power over ordinary legislation. Often layered over an elected apparatus, producing two competing sources of mandate.

Dominant loop
Doctrinal authority validates rulers; rulers protect doctrinal authority.
Characteristic failure
Tying governance outcomes to sacred legitimacy means governance failures become theological crises.

Hybrid / anocracy

W: mixed

Partly open, partly closed; competing institutions with no settled hierarchy. Not a way-station so much as its own stable-unstable condition.

Dominant loop
Contested rules invite each faction to change the rules rather than play by them.
Characteristic failure
Empirically the highest risk of internal armed conflict — higher than either consolidated democracies or consolidated autocracies.

Stateless and pre-state orders

W: varies

Segmentary lineage societies, clan and tribal confederations, feudal webs of personal obligation: order without a monopoly on violence. Disputes settled by kin liability, feud, elders, or reciprocal obligation rather than courts.

Dominant loop
Reciprocity and reputation; deterrence through credible retaliation.
Characteristic failure
Scales badly beyond face-to-face monitoring; vulnerable to any neighbour that has centralised.
Why it matters
Shows the state is one solution to the political problem, not the problem's definition.

Empire

W: tiny at the core

Indirect rule over heterogeneous units that keep their own local systems, bound to a centre by tribute, garrisons and elite co-option rather than uniform citizenship.

Dominant loop
Co-opt local elites cheaply; they enforce on your behalf.
Characteristic failure
Overextension — the cost of holding the periphery outruns what the periphery yields.

Direct democracy & sortition

W: wide

Citizens decide themselves rather than through representatives. Classical Athens filled most offices by lottery, not election — on the view that elections select for wealth and rhetoric while lottery selects a genuine cross-section. Survives today in Swiss referendums and modern citizens' assemblies.

Dominant loop
Participation builds competence, which sustains participation.
Characteristic failure
Agenda control and attention scarcity; expertise gaps; majority decisions with no rights floor.

Supranational pooling

W: indirect

States delegate specific powers upward to a shared body while keeping sovereignty formally. The EU is the deep case; the WTO, and treaty regimes generally, are thinner ones.

Dominant loop
Integration creates interdependence, which raises the cost of leaving, which deepens integration.
Characteristic failure
Deliberately many veto players → immobility; and a legitimacy gap, since citizens elect national governments but are governed by the pooled body.
Read the labels with suspicion Regime names describe formal structure; the dimensions describe how power actually moves. Many systems that share a label behave nothing alike, and many that carry opposite labels behave identically. When the label and the dimensions disagree, trust the dimensions.

Four results that only show up at this scale

Michels' iron law of oligarchy

Robert Michels studied socialist parties — organisations explicitly committed to internal democracy — and found they became oligarchic anyway. Not from bad faith: from specialisation. A few people accumulate expertise, contacts, files and time. Everyone else has a day job. "Who says organisation, says oligarchy."

The claim is contested as a law, but the mechanism is real, and it is the strongest argument for deliberately cheap information and rotating roles.

Forking makes dictatorship survivable

Open-source projects tolerate benevolent dictators that no nation would accept. Why? Because exit costs almost nothing: fork the repo and take the community. The constraint on the dictator is not voice, it is the permanent credible threat of exit.

Generalise it: the cheaper exit is, the less voice machinery you need — and the more dangerous it is to make exit expensive without building voice to replace it.

Mafias as private states

Diego Gambetta's account of the Sicilian mafia treats it as an industry supplying protection where the state's supply was absent or untrusted. Contracts need enforcement; if no public enforcer is credible, a private one gets paid.

The lesson is not about crime. It is that governance gaps get filled — by someone, on their terms. The same is true of undefined ownership inside an organisation.

Anarchy is a structure, not a vacuum

In international relations "anarchy" means only that there is no higher authority. Order still emerges — from balance of power, from one dominant actor providing public goods, or from institutions states build to make their own promises credible.

The recurring difficulty is the commitment problem: I would sign, but I cannot be sure you will still comply once circumstances shift and nobody can make you.

Ostrom's eight conditions — what actually works for shared resources

Elinor Ostrom won the Nobel for showing that communities do successfully govern shared resources without either privatisation or central control — and for extracting the conditions under which they manage it. These are the most directly reusable rules on this page.

2Rules fit local conditionsImported rules that ignore local reality get ignored back.

08 — Systems

Stocks, flows and the delays that do the damage

Here is where the systems frame earns its place. Stop looking at events — an election, a protest, a resignation — and look at the accumulations underneath them. Events are the surface; stocks explain the surface.

The stocks

Legitimacy

Fills slowly, over years of delivered promises and kept procedures. Drains fast, and non-linearly. Cannot be bought with a campaign; can be destroyed with one broken rule at the wrong moment.

State capacity

Three sub-stocks: fiscal (can it tax?), administrative (can it deliver?), coercive (can it enforce?). Built over decades; degraded in a few years of neglect or purges. Many regimes have plenty of the third and almost none of the second.

Social trust

Whether strangers expect cooperation. Determines how much enforcement any given rule needs. It is the reason identical laws produce different outcomes in different societies.

Accumulated grievance

Doesn't dissipate on its own; it waits. This is why an apparently trivial trigger can produce an enormous response — the trigger released a stock, it didn't create one.

Institutional memory

The rule stock plus the people who know why the rules exist. Purges, high turnover and reorganisation drain it invisibly — you only discover it is gone when you need it.

Economic surplus

What is available to distribute. Growing surplus makes distribution problems feel like coordination problems. Shrinking surplus turns every ordinary decision back into a fight.

Why stocks matter more than events A regime's apparent stability tells you about the flows — today's compliance, this week's calm. Its actual fragility lives in the stocks. This is why collapses look sudden: the stock had been draining for years while every observable flow looked normal, right up until it crossed a threshold.

The delay that explains "short-termism"

The most consequential structural fact about democratic governance is not corruption or incompetence. It is a mismatch between two clocks: the payoff period of institutional investment, and the period over which decision-makers are judged.

The standard fix Every system that has partly solved this used the same move: split the fast institution from the slow one. Independent central banks, career civil services, constitutional courts, ring-fenced infrastructure funds, statutory auditors. Each removes a slow-payoff decision from the fast clock and gives it its own tenure. Every one of them buys long-horizon capability at the price of democratic responsiveness — that trade is the argument, and it is a real one, not a settled one.
09 — Systems

The governing loops

Six loops explain most political dynamics anywhere. One is balancing — it corrects error. Five are reinforcing — they amplify whatever they start with. A system's trajectory is decided by which loops are dominant and what limits them.

B1 · balancing

The accountability loop

Poor outcomes → visible dissatisfaction → leaders replaced → policy corrected → outcomes improve.

This is the only error-correcting loop in the set, and it is fragile. It needs three things at once: information people can act on, real alternatives to switch to, and a safe transfer of power so losing is survivable. Break any one and the loop opens.

B2 · balancing

The guardrail loop

Overreach → independent court, press, auditor or federal unit pushes back → overreach checked.

Constitutional guardrails are, mechanically, negative feedback devices. Their strength is what decides whether the reinforcing loops below run away. Which is why the first move of anyone intending to run away with a system is to weaken them — usually through appointments and budgets rather than abolition.

R1 · reinforcing

The patronage trap

Narrow coalition → loyalty bought with private goods → public goods and growth decline → less surplus → coalition must narrow further to stay affordable.

Drawn in full in §05. A poverty trap with a political engine.

R2 · reinforcing

The repression trap

Dissent → repression → grievance rises and legitimacy falls → more dissent → more repression.

With a vicious second arc: each round raises the cost of losing power for the ruler, since anyone who has done enough cannot safely step down. The system loses its own exit. Escalation is then the only remaining strategy, regardless of preference.

R3 · reinforcing

The capture loop

Concentrated benefits, diffuse costs → lobbying pays → policy favours incumbents → incumbents grow richer → more lobbying capacity.

Mancur Olson's later argument: stable, undisturbed societies gradually accumulate distributional coalitions that make them rigid. Long stability is not free — it silts up.

R4 · reinforcing

The polarisation loop

Competition plus attention incentives → identities sort onto one axis → the other side reads as a threat → norm-breaking by your own side becomes acceptable → the other side reciprocates.

Once losing an election feels existential rather than inconvenient, the guardrail loop stops being defended by anyone — each side sees the guardrail as an obstacle to stopping the other.

R5 — the information trap, and why it is the most transferable

This loop runs in autocracies, in armies, in large companies, and in any team whose leader flinches at bad news. It is the one worth internalising, because unlike the others you can be inside it without anything looking wrong.

The companion effect Timur Kuran's preference falsification: when saying what you think is costly, people publicly state a preference they privately reject. Everyone may oppose the arrangement while each believes they are alone. Support looks total right until some small event reveals that others feel the same — and then a cascade flips the public position of an entire population in weeks. This is why revolutions blindside even the intelligence services that were watching for them: they were measuring stated preferences, and the real variable was common knowledge, which is unobservable until the moment it isn't. The same mechanism runs in any meeting where disagreement is expensive.

11 — Systems

Where the leverage is

Donella Meadows ranked intervention points by how much change they produce per unit of effort. Applied to political systems, the ranking is uncomfortable: the interventions people argue about most are near the bottom, and the ones near the top are rarely on the agenda at all.

The meta-rule is the real prize In any system, the rule that governs how rules change — the amendment clause, the reorg authority, who may rewrite the process document — sits above every ordinary rule. Fights over it look procedural and are not. If you can only watch one thing in a system you are trying to understand, watch who can change the rules and how hard it is for them.
12 — Applied

The same machine, at work

An organisation is a political system that has agreed not to call itself one. It has a selectorate, veto players, a legitimacy story, an enforcement apparatus, a succession problem and a constitution — all of them unnamed, which makes them harder to see and no less real. Here is the dictionary.

Seven consequences that actually change what you do

Writing the document beats arguing in the meeting

McKelvey's chaos theorem in its office form: when a decision has several dimensions — cost, speed, risk, morale — the person who sets the order of questions can steer the group to nearly any outcome, one reasonable-looking step at a time. Whoever frames the options has already made most of the decision. If you are only ever responding to someone else's document, you are playing a weaker position than you think.

Count veto players, not supporters

Change does not require a majority; it requires clearing every actor who can independently block. Six enthusiastic supporters and one unconsulted security reviewer is a dead proposal. Map the veto set at the start, and spend your effort on the blockers rather than on deepening the enthusiasm of people who already agree.

A shrinking coalition produces favour-trading, predictably

When only one person's opinion decides outcomes, the rational strategy for everyone else becomes pleasing that person rather than improving results — exactly the private-goods dynamic from §05. It is not a character failing in the people involved; it is what the structure pays for. The fix is structural too: widen who has a real say in the judgement, and make the criteria legible in advance.

If bad news is expensive, your data becomes decoration

The information trap does not need a tyrant — a visible flicker of irritation at a red metric is enough to start it. Two counters: reward the person who brought the bad number visibly and in the room, and build at least one channel where the number arrives without passing through anyone whose performance it reflects.

Silence in a meeting is not agreement

Preference falsification runs hardest where disagreement is costly and everyone can see everyone. If you need to know what the room actually thinks, do not ask the room — collect positions privately first, then publish the distribution. The cascade dynamic also means a team can look entirely aligned right up to the week three people resign together.

Your review cadence is an electoral cycle, with the same pathology

Quarterly judgement plus multi-year payoffs produces short-termism for structural reasons, not because anyone is lazy. Use the standard fix: split the slow institution from the fast one. Protected long-horizon budget, an architecture or platform commitment that survives a quarter's noise, a separate review track judged on leading indicators rather than outcomes that cannot yet exist.

Use Ostrom for anything shared

A shared platform, a common codebase, a joint metric, an on-call rota — these are commons, and the eight conditions in §07 apply directly. Clear boundaries on who owns what. Graduated sanctions rather than a policy nobody dares invoke. Conflict resolution cheap enough that people use it instead of routing around. Rules changeable by the people bound by them — which is where most internal platform governance quietly fails.

Reading an unfamiliar organisation in twenty minutes Five questions, in order. 1. Who is actually in the winning coalition for the decision you care about? 2. Who can block, and on what grounds? 3. What is the legitimacy story — is it the data, the process, the seniority, or the founder? 4. What happens to the person who brings bad news? 5. Who can change the process itself? Those five answers predict more of what will happen than any org chart will.
One honest caution This frame is diagnostic, and it is easy to misuse. Seeing an organisation as a power system explains why sensible proposals die and why identical people behave differently under different structures — that is real, and worth having. But treating every interaction as positional is both exhausting and usually wrong: most colleagues most of the time are trying to do the work. Use the frame when something structural is happening and the ordinary explanation has already failed.
13 — Applied

What this frame does not settle

A systems account tells you how the machinery behaves. It does not tell you what the machinery should be for, and it is worth being precise about the gap rather than letting the analysis quietly pose as an answer.

It does not rank the values. Liberty, equality, order, community, national self-determination and material prosperity genuinely conflict, and no amount of loop analysis adjudicates between them. Two people can agree on every mechanism here and still disagree completely about which trade-off to make — and that disagreement is not a failure of reasoning on either side.

It under-weights ideas. The account above treats legitimacy as a stock, which is useful and incomplete. People have died for constitutional ideas they could have abandoned at no personal cost. Nationalism, religion, rights, dignity — these are causal forces in their own right, not just fuel for a loop, and the model is thinner where they matter most.

It is quiet about who counts. "Preferences get aggregated" hides the prior question of whose preferences enter at all. The most consequential political changes in modern history were expansions of the selectorate, and the frame describes those as parameter changes when they were something far larger.

Structure is not destiny. Identical institutional designs produce wildly different results in different places, because history, geography, sequencing and sheer contingency all bite. The dimensions predict tendencies, not outcomes. Anyone offering you a clean causal law about regimes is selling something.

And the empirical questions are genuinely open. Whether democracy causes growth or growth causes democracy; whether presidential systems really are more failure-prone or merely appeared in harder places; whether polarisation is driven by media, inequality or party structure — these are live, contested research questions with serious people on both sides. Where this page states a mechanism confidently, treat the mechanism as well-evidenced and the magnitude as arguable.
