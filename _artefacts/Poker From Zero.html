Part 0

What poker actually is

Almost every beginner starts with a wrong model of the game, so let’s kill it immediately.

The wrong model

Poker is a game where you get dealt cards, and the person with the best cards wins the money.

The right model

Poker is a betting game. Cards are just the raw material the bets are made of.

Here is the proof, and it is the single most important paragraph in this document.

Two players, Anita and Bharat. Over 100 hands, Anita is dealt better cards every single time. She wins 90 hands and loses 10. But on the 90 she wins, she gets scared and bets tiny, winning ₹100 each. On the 10 she loses, she refuses to give up and pays ₹1,500 each. Anita won 90% of the hands and lost ₹6,000. Bharat had worse cards all night and is up ₹6,000.

Read that again. You are not trying to win hands. You are trying to win money. These are different goals, and confusing them is the root cause of maybe 70% of all losing poker.

Which leads to the definition we will use for the rest of the guide:

Poker is the skill of putting money in when you have the better of it, and getting away cheaply when you don’t — under conditions where you can never be sure which is which.

Every technique in this document — hand rankings, position, pot odds, bluffing, bankroll — is a tool serving that one sentence.

Why this game is even solvable

Consider two extremes.

Chess is a game of complete information. Both players see everything. There are no secrets. If you are better, you win, near-always.

A coin flip is a game of no information. Nobody can be better at it. Skill is impossible.

Poker sits between them: incomplete information. You know your own two cards. You do not know your opponent’s. But you are not blind either — you get clues, and the clues are the bets themselves.

This is the whole reason poker is a skill game. Your edge does not come from seeing more cards than your opponent. It comes from drawing better conclusions from the same limited clues, and making better priced bets on those conclusions. In this respect poker is far closer to business decisions, insurance pricing, or investing than it is to a slot machine.

The system diagram

Hold this picture in your head. Everything else in this guide hangs off it.

Notice the two boxes you control and the two you don’t. You control your situation (by choosing which hands to play and from where) and your decision. You control neither the cards nor the other human.

Also notice that dangerous bottom box. A bad player loses money. A doomed player loses money and then updates their strategy based on those losses, because they can’t tell luck from skill. We’ll come back to this in Part 11 — it’s the most important part of the guide and the part people skip.

Part 1

The five truths the entire game is built on

Learn these five and you can rederive most poker strategy yourself.

Truth 1You win chips, not hands

Already covered, but it has a practical corollary that surprises people:

A winning player folds most of the hands they are dealt. Typically 70–80% of them, before even seeing a single shared card.

That feels wrong — surely being aggressive is how you win? But think about it as a cost problem. Playing a hand costs money. Playing a bad hand costs money and gives you nothing back. Folding costs you almost nothing (just the forced bet, which we’ll get to). So the cheapest way to make money is to not play the hands where you’re the underdog.

Beginners lose most of their money not through dramatic bluffs, but through a slow drip of playing too many hands.

Truth 2Every bet is a price, and you compare it to a chance

This is the mathematical heart of poker, and it is genuinely simple.

Situation: There is ₹300 in the middle. Your opponent bets ₹100. You must put in ₹100 to keep playing. You believe you have roughly a 1-in-5 chance of ending up with the best hand.

Should you call?

Don’t guess. Count.

  • If you call, the middle will contain ₹300 + ₹100 (theirs) + ₹100 (yours) = ₹500
  • You are risking ₹100 to win ₹500
  • So you need to be right more than 100 ÷ 500 = 20% of the time to break even

You said you win 20% of the time. That’s exactly break-even. At 25% it’s a clear call; at 15% it’s a clear fold.

That’s it. That’s the math. Cost of the call ÷ (total pot after your call) = the win rate you need. You will do this calculation a thousand times. It is called pot odds, and Parts 4 and 5 drill it.

The mental shift: stop asking “do I have a good hand?” — a meaningless question — and start asking “am I getting a good price?” A weak hand at a great price is a call. A strong hand at a terrible price is a fold.

Truth 3Information is unequal, and the seating decides who gets it

In poker, players act one at a time, in a fixed clockwise order. That order gets rotated by one seat every hand.

Consequence: on any given hand, some players must act before seeing what others do, and one player acts after everyone else.

Acting last is enormously valuable. When you act last you have seen everyone else’s decision; they have seen nothing of yours. You are making a decision with more facts.

How much is it worth? Roughly: the same two cards are worth two to three times more money when you’re last to act than when you’re first. Same cards. Different seat. That’s not a small effect — it’s one of the largest in the game, and it is almost entirely invisible to beginners, who look only at their cards.

Part 6 is entirely about this.

Truth 4You’re not playing against a hand, you’re playing against a set of possible hands

The single biggest upgrade from beginner to intermediate thinking.

The beginner asks: “What does he have? I bet he has an ace.”

That question is unanswerable and the guess is worthless.

The better question: “Given everything he has done this hand, what is the full list of hands he could have? And within that list, how does my hand do?”

Example. A tight, careful player raises before any shared cards appear. What does he have? You cannot know. But you can say: a player like this raises from that seat with roughly the top 10% of possible starting hands. That’s a list of about 20 specific combinations — big pairs, big aces, a few strong suited hands.

Now the shared cards come out: 7♣7♦2♠.

He bets. Now reason across the whole list:

So roughly: some of his list is strong, a large chunk of it is now air. This tells you something concrete and actionable, without you ever needing to “know what he has.”

This way of thinking is called putting someone on a range — just plain-English “the set of hands they could have.” Once you think in ranges, you stop being surprised, you stop feeling cheated by the cards, and you start making decisions that are correct on average rather than correct in your imagination.

Truth 5Results and decisions are different things, and the short run is a liar

The hardest truth, and the one that decides whether you ever get good.

Poker has a large random component. The consequence is brutal:

  • You can make a perfect decision and lose the money.
  • You can make a terrible decision and win the money.

Both happen constantly. So you cannot use “did I win?” as feedback on “did I play well?” That signal is buried in noise, and it takes an enormous number of hands to dig out.

Scale of the problem, roughly: a genuinely good online player might earn a small edge per hand. The random swing around that edge is so much larger than the edge itself that a strong winning player can lose money over 20,000 hands, and a bad player can win over 20,000 hands. Twenty thousand hands is months of casual play.

So: judge your decisions on their logic, not their outcome. After a hand you lost, the only useful question is “given what I knew at the time, was that the right price?” If yes, you played it correctly and you should do it again next time. If you instead conclude “that didn’t work, I’ll stop doing it,” you have just let randomness rewrite your strategy — and that is the loop in the bottom of the diagram, eating your money.

This is also why good players talk about variance and bankroll constantly. Not out of pessimism, but because a strategy that is correct on average will still go through long stretches of being wrong, and you must survive those stretches to collect. Part 11 covers this properly.

Part 2

The mechanics: how a hand of Texas Hold’em actually works

This part is pure rules. No strategy. Read it once and you can physically play.

The setup

  • Number of players: 2 to 10. Online, the two common formats are 6-max (six seats) and full ring (nine seats). Start with 6-max — more action, fewer players to think about.
  • Deck: one standard 52-card deck. No jokers.
  • What you get: two private cards, face down, that only you see. These are called your hole cards.
  • What everyone shares: up to five cards dealt face up in the middle of the table. These are the board (or “community cards”). Everybody uses them.

Your final hand is the best five cards you can make out of your two private cards plus the five shared ones — seven cards total, pick the best five.

You are never forced to use both your cards. You can use two, one, or even zero of them.

The two forced bets (this is the engine of the game)

If nobody was ever forced to bet, everyone would just fold every hand forever and nothing would happen. So two players are forced to put money in blind, before seeing their cards. These are the blinds.

  • Big blind (BB): the full forced bet. This is the unit everything is measured in. If the big blind is ₹2, then “a ₹100 stack” is called “50 big blinds.”
  • Small blind (SB): half of it.

The forced bets rotate one seat clockwise every hand, so everyone pays equally over time. The blinds are why you must play. They are a small clock ticking against you, forcing you to win pots to stay even.

The table map

Seats are named by their relationship to the button — a marker that shows who is the nominal dealer. The button moves one seat clockwise each hand.

Your four possible actions

At any point when it’s your turn, you have at most four choices. That’s the entire game.

A betting round ends when everybody still in has either folded, or put in the same amount.

All-in: if a bet is larger than the chips in front of you, you can still call for everything you have. You can’t lose more than what’s on the table. You can never be forced to reach into your pocket mid-hand.

The four betting rounds

A hand of Hold’em is four rounds of betting with cards revealed between them. Information increases as you go; so do the bet sizes.

Two ways the hand can end:

  1. Everyone folds but one person. That person takes the pot and never has to show their cards. This is why bluffing exists — you can win with anything if everyone else quits.
  2. Showdown. Two or more players are still in after the river. Cards go face up, best five-card hand wins. If two hands tie exactly, the pot is split.

A full hand, narrated

Six players. Blinds are ₹1 (small) and ₹2 (big). Everyone has ₹200 in front of them (100 big blinds). You are on the button — the best seat.

Preflop. ₹3 is already in the middle from the two blinds. You look down at A♠K♠ — ace and king, both spades. A very strong start.

  • UTG folds. HJ folds. CO folds.
  • Your turn. You raise to ₹5. (Standard opening size: about 2.5× the big blind.)
  • Small blind folds — his forced ₹1 stays in the pot. Big blind calls ₹5 (she puts in ₹3 more, since ₹2 was already forced in).

Pot: ₹11. Two players left. You will act last for the rest of the hand.

Flop. Dealer turns over three cards: A♦8♣3♥

Your best five cards right now: A♠A♦K♠8♣3♥ → a pair of aces, with a king alongside. Very strong on this board.

  • She acts first (she’s in the blind). She checks.
  • You bet ₹7.
  • She calls ₹7.

Pot: ₹25.

Why did you bet? Two reasons at once: worse hands might call and pay you (someone with 8-something, or a lower pair), and you don’t want to give a free card to a hand that could catch up. Both reasons point the same way, so betting is easy.

Turn. Fourth shared card: A♦8♣3♥9♠

You still have a pair of aces. She checks again. You bet ₹18. She calls.

Pot: ₹61.

What’s happening in her head? You now have information. She has checked and called twice. Players who call twice without ever raising usually hold something decent but not huge — a middling pair, or a hand that needs one more card. She almost certainly does not have three aces; she’d have raised somewhere.

River. Final card: A♦8♣3♥9♠2♣

Nothing changed. You still have a pair of aces plus a king.

  • She checks.
  • You bet ₹35.
  • She thinks, then calls.

Showdown. She turns over 8♥7♥ — a pair of eights. You turn over A♠K♠ — a pair of aces. You win the ₹131 pot. You put in ₹65 across the hand, so your profit is ₹66 — roughly 33 big blinds, a very good hand.

What actually earned that money

Notice how little of it was the cards.

  1. You only played a hand worth playing. Three players folded before you. They were dealt cards too.
  2. You raised, so the pot was already bigger than the forced bets before any skill was involved.
  3. You had position. You acted last on every single round. She had to reveal her intent first, three times in a row, for free.
  4. You bet three times instead of politely checking. She paid you ₹7, ₹18 and ₹35 — ₹60 of the ₹66 profit came from those three bets. A timid player who checks it down and wins at showdown collects the ₹11 pot instead. That difference — ₹66 versus ₹6 — is the whole game.
  5. You increased your bet as the pot grew. Bets are sized relative to the pot, not plucked from the air.

That hand is a template. Most of your profit will look exactly like that: unremarkable, un-dramatic, and mostly earned before the cards even came out.

Part 3

Hand rankings, and why they’re in that order

Here’s the ranking. But do not memorise the list blindly, because there’s a rule underneath it that makes it obvious:

Rarer hands beat commoner hands. That’s the whole logic. Nothing else.

There are exactly 2,598,960 different five-card hands in a 52-card deck. The ranking is simply those hands sorted by how few ways there are to make them.

The table

Strongest at the top.

Sanity check on the logic: a flush (5,108 ways) beats a straight (10,200 ways) because it’s about half as common. That’s the only reason. There’s no deeper meaning to it.

The four things beginners get wrong here

1Suits have no ranking

Hearts do not beat spades. A flush in hearts and a flush in spades of the same numbers is an exact tie. Suits only matter for making a flush. Nothing else, ever.

2Aces are both the highest and the lowest card

An ace can be the top of a straight (10-J-Q-K-A, the best straight) or the bottom (A-2-3-4-5, the worst straight, sometimes called “the wheel”). It cannot wrap around: Q-K-A-2-3 is not a straight. It’s nothing.

3When both have the same hand, the side cards decide

This decides more pots than anything else, and it’s where new players lose money invisibly.

Board: A♥9♣4♦3♠2♥

You hold A♠7♦ → pair of aces, and your best five are A A 9 7 4

He holds A♣K♦ → pair of aces, and his best five are A A K 9 4

Both have a pair of aces. His K beats your 7. He wins the whole pot.

That extra deciding card is called a kicker. This is why A-K is far better than A-7, even though both are “an ace.” When an ace hits the board and two players both have one, the guy with the bigger second card takes everything. There is no partial credit.

Compare only in order: highest card first, then next, then next, until they differ. If all five are identical in rank, it’s a true split pot.

4The board can beat your hand — or make everyone equal

Board: 8♠8♥8♦K♣K♥

You hold 2♣3♦.

Your best five cards are 8-8-8-K-K — a full house! But so are his, and everyone else’s. The pot splits. Your two cards contributed nothing.

Also, mildly embarrassing but important: if the board is 9♠10♠J♠Q♠K♠, everyone at the table has a royal flush and everyone splits. Your cards can’t make it better.

Part 4

Counting: how likely is my hand to get there?

Before you can compare price to chance (Truth 2), you need the chance. Here’s how to get it in about four seconds, in your head, with no training.

Step 1Count your “outs”

An out is simply a card still unseen that would give you a winning hand.

Count them. Literally count.

Example. You hold 7♥8♥. Board: 2♥K♥9♣

Right now you have nothing — no pair, no straight. But you have four hearts. One more heart gives you a flush.

There are 13 hearts in a deck. You can see 4 of them. So 9 hearts remain unseen. You have 9 outs.

Example 2. You hold J♠10♦. Board: 9♥8♣2♠

You need a Queen or a 7 to complete a straight (Q-J-10-9-8, or J-10-9-8-7). Four queens plus four sevens = 8 outs.

Example 3. You hold A♣Q♣. Board: 7♦4♠2♥

You have nothing at all. What would help? An ace or a queen would give you the best pair. 3 aces + 3 queens = 6 outs. (Only 3 each, because you’re holding one of each.)

Step 2The rule of 2 and 4

You don’t need a calculator. Use this:

×4

Multiply your outs by 4 if two more cards are coming (you’re on the flop, and you’ll see both turn and river).

×2

Multiply your outs by 2 if one more card is coming (you’re on the turn, only the river left).

The answer is your rough percentage.

Check it against reality:

Close enough for every practical purpose. One caveat: with 9 or more outs, the ×4 version runs a bit optimistic — knock a few points off. And ×4 is only honest if you’re actually going to see both remaining cards, which usually means the money’s already all in. If your opponent gets to bet again on the turn, you’ll have to pay again, so mentally use something between ×2 and ×4.

Step 3Put it together with the price

Now Truth 2 comes alive. Full worked example:

Pot is ₹100. You hold 7♥8♥, board is 2♥K♥9♣ — 9 outs to a flush.

Opponent bets ₹50.

The chance: 9 outs, one card at a time — but let’s say we’ll see the turn and probably pay again. Call it ~20% for the turn card alone.

The price: you put in ₹50. Pot becomes 100 + 50 + 50 = ₹200. You risk ₹50 to win ₹200 → you need 25%.

Verdict: 20% chance, 25% needed. Strictly, that’s a fold.

But wait — two things the raw numbers miss:

  1. What happens when you hit? If a heart lands, you’ll likely win more money on the next round, not just the current pot. Your true reward is bigger than ₹200. This is called implied odds, and it’s why drawing hands like suited connectors are playable — the payoff when they hit is huge relative to the cost.
  2. Position. If you’re acting last, you get to see whether he bets again before deciding, which is worth real money. If you’re first, you don’t.

With those in, this call is roughly break-even — fine in position, slightly bad out of position. That is a typical poker decision: not obviously right or wrong, decided by small factors, worth a few rupees either way. Poker is thousands of those, not a handful of dramatic bluffs.

The dark twin: reverse implied odds

Implied odds means “I might win extra later.” Its evil twin means “I might lose extra later.”

You hold K♦9♣. Board: K♠7♥4♦. You have top pair.

Sounds great. But think about who keeps betting into you. If he has a worse king (K-3, K-5) you win a little. If he has A-K, K-Q, K-J, two pair, or three of a kind, you lose a lot — because you’ll happily pay him off for three rounds with what feels like a strong hand.

Hands with big reverse implied odds — weak kickers, second-best-looking hands, “top pair no kicker” — are how good players extract money from beginners. They don’t need to bluff you. They just need you to have K-9 while they have A-K.

Practical rule: be more scared of the hands that look strong but are easily beaten by a similar hand, than of hands that are obviously weak. Obviously weak hands you fold, and folding is free. It’s the second-best hand that empties your account.

Part 5

Expected value: the one formula

Everything above is a shortcut to this. Here’s the actual engine.

Expected value (EV) = (chance of winning × amount you win) − (chance of losing × amount you lose)

If it’s positive, do it. If it’s negative, don’t. Over enough repetitions, your money converges to the sum of your EVs. That is a mathematical fact, and it is the only reason skill in poker translates to money.

Worked example — a call

Pot ₹100, he bets ₹50, you have a 30% chance to win.

  • Win: 30% of the time you take ₹150 (the ₹100 pot + his ₹50)
  • Lose: 70% of the time you lose your ₹50

EV = (0.30 × 150) − (0.70 × 50) = 45 − 35 = +₹10

Call. Not because you’ll win — you’ll lose this specific hand 70% of the time — but because doing it a thousand times earns ₹10,000. This is the mental habit that separates poker players from gamblers: you are indifferent to this hand, and entirely focused on the average.

Worked example — a bluff

You have nothing. Pot is ₹100. You’re thinking of betting ₹50 to make him fold.

  • If he folds: you win ₹100.
  • If he calls: you lose ₹50.

Break-even point: you need him to fold X of the time where 100X = 50(1−X) → X = 33%.

The general rule, worth memorising: a bluff of size B into a pot of P needs to work B ÷ (P + B) of the time.

Two enormous consequences fall out of this table:

1. Small bluffs are cheap. A half-pot bluff only needs to work one time in three. Against an opponent who folds when he misses — and he misses two-thirds of flops, remember Part 3 — that is an easy bar to clear. This is why aggression makes money.

2. Big bluffs need a specific reason. A double-pot shove needs to work two times in three. That’s a strong claim about your opponent. Don’t make it without evidence.

Where the beginner’s intuition fails

The beginner thinks: “I shouldn’t bluff, I might get caught.” But getting caught is priced in. A bluff that works 50% of the time and gets caught 50% of the time is profitable at half-pot sizing. Getting caught is not failure; it’s the cost of doing business, already in the budget.

Conversely: “I have to call, he might be bluffing.” Also priced in. If you need 33% and he’s only bluffing 20% of the time, folding is correct even though you know he sometimes bluffs. Being right occasionally isn’t the standard. Being right often enough is.

Part 6

Position: the free money nobody sees

Truth 3 said acting last is worth 2–3× on the same cards. Let’s prove it, because once you feel why, you’ll never forget it.

Why last is so strong

Four separate advantages, stacking on top of each other:

1. You get a free look at their intention. They must commit to check or bet before you decide anything. Every hand, every round. That’s four free readings per hand, and you give none back.

2. You control the price. If you want the pot to grow, you bet. If you want it to stay small, you check. Out of position, your opponent decides the price and you only get to accept or refuse.

3. Your bluffs work better. He checks — that’s mild evidence he’s weak. Now your bluff is aimed at a target you already know is soft. Out of position, you have to bluff blind, into someone who might be about to raise.

4. You can end the hand. Acting last, checking closes the round and gets you a free card. Out of position, checking just invites a bet you’ll have to answer.

Feel it with an example

Same two cards. 9♥8♥. Board: A♠7♦2♣. You have nothing.

Scenario A — you act first. You have to guess. Check and he’ll probably bet, and you fold, having learned nothing. Bet, and you might run into his ace. You’re making decisions in the dark.

Scenario B — you act last. He checks. You now know he probably doesn’t have an ace either (most people bet an ace here). You bet small. He folds. You just won a pot with the worst hand, using zero cards and one piece of free information.

That pot exists only because of where you were sitting. That’s what “position is worth 2–3×” actually means in practice.

The rule this generates

Play more hands when you’re late to act. Play fewer when you’re early.

That’s the entire practical takeaway, and it is directly responsible for the starting-hand chart in the next section. The chart isn’t arbitrary; it’s Truth 3 written out as a table.

The blinds: a trap in plain sight

The small and big blind act last before the flop, which fools beginners into thinking they’re good seats. They are the worst seats at the table, because they act first on the flop, turn and river — three rounds out of four, with the most money in play.

Add the forced bet and you get the standard result: the blinds are losing positions for everyone, including the best players in the world. The goal in the blinds is not to profit; it’s to lose less than the person next to you. Fold more, call less, and don’t get attached to hands just because you already have money in.

Part 7

Which hands to play

Reading hand shorthand

Three symbols and you can read any poker discussion:

  • T means 10. (So “T9s” is ten-nine.)
  • s means suited — both your cards the same suit. A5s = ace and five of the same suit.
  • o means offsuit — different suits. A5o = ace and five, different suits.
  • A5s+ means “A5s and everything above it in that family” — A5s, A6s, A7s… up to AKs.
  • 77+ means 77, 88, 99, TT, JJ, QQ, KK, AA.

There are 1,326 possible two-card combinations, but only 169 meaningfully different starting hands (since which particular spade you hold doesn’t matter). That’s a manageable list.

Why suited and connected cards matter more than they look

Two structural facts, both from Part 3’s table:

Suited is worth about 2–3% in raw equity — small. But its real value is the shape of the outcomes. When a suited hand wins, it tends to win a big pot (a flush is hard to fold against), and when it loses, it tends to lose a small one (you missed and folded cheaply). Big wins, small losses. That asymmetry is worth far more than the 2–3% suggests.

Connected cards (7-8, J-10) make straights. Same asymmetry: rarely, and expensively for the opponent.

A-6 offsuit is a trap hand. It looks like it has an ace in it, so beginners play it, and then they hit an ace and lose a big pot to A-K. A hand that wins small and loses big is worse than a hand that just loses. That’s the reverse-implied-odds lesson again.

Part 8

Betting: there are only two reasons to put money in

This section will do more for your game than the hand chart.

Every bet you ever make must be for one of exactly two reasons:

1

VALUEyou think you’re ahead, and you want worse hands to call you.

2

BLUFFyou think you’re behind, and you want better hands to fold.

That’s the complete list. Two items.

Before every bet, ask: which of the two is this? If you can’t answer, don’t bet — check instead. That single discipline will save you more money than any other habit in poker.

The bets that don’t have a reason

Beginners make three kinds of reasonless bets. Learn to catch yourself:

“I want to see where I’m at.” You’re paying money for information you can get for free by checking. And the information is unreliable anyway. This is the most expensive habit in amateur poker.

“I want to protect my hand.” Understandable instinct, but usually confused. If you’re so far ahead that nothing can catch you, protection is pointless. If you’re behind, protection is impossible. The narrow real case — “I’m ahead but vulnerable” — is actually reason 1, so just call it value and size it properly.

“It felt like the right size.” Bets should be a fraction of the pot, chosen deliberately. More below.

Sizing: think in fractions of the pot, never in absolute money

Because Part 5 showed the break-even math depends entirely on bet-to-pot ratio, your bet size is a mathematical statement. Choose it on purpose.

A crucial habit: use the same size for your value bets and your bluffs on the same board. If you bet big only with monsters and small only with bluffs, observant opponents will read you like a book within twenty minutes. Consistent sizing makes you unreadable. It costs you nothing and is the cheapest defence there is.

The continuation bet — your bread and butter

After you raise before the flop and get one caller, the flop comes and it’s your turn. Betting here — regardless of whether you hit — is called a continuation bet, and it’s the most common profitable play in poker.

Why it works, in numbers:

  • You raised, so your opponent believes you have a strong range. That belief is free to you.
  • The flop misses him roughly two-thirds of the time (Part 3).
  • A half-pot bluff needs to work 33% of the time (Part 5).
  • He folds more than 33% when he’s missed. Therefore: profit.

That’s it. The whole play is those four lines. You don’t need a good hand; you need the story to be plausible and the price to be right.

When to fire it, and when not to

The general principle: bluff on boards that help few hands. Slow down on boards that help many. This is called reading “texture,” and it’s just counting how many of his possible holdings improved.

Betting on the later rounds

Each round the pot is bigger, so each bet is bigger in absolute terms. This means the river bet is where the real money is won and lost. A pot that starts at ₹10 preflop can hold ₹300 by the river; the last bet is worth thirty times the first.

Two river principles:

Value-bet thinner than feels comfortable. If you think you’re ahead of the hands that would call, bet — even with a medium hand. Most beginners check the river with a decent hand out of fear, and leave money uncollected on every single one.

When you’re bluffing the river, tell a consistent story. Ask: “if I actually had the hand I’m pretending to have, would I have played it exactly like this?” If the answer is no, your bluff is much less likely to work. The bet has to be the last chapter of a believable story, not a random act of aggression.

Part 9

Reading opponents when you can’t see their face

Online, there is no face, no shaking hands, no nervous chip-fiddling. Good. Physical tells are overrated anyway and mostly noise. What you get online is better: precise, countable behaviour, over hundreds of hands.

The four player types

Two axes. How many hands does he play (loose vs tight)? And how does he play them (passively — checking and calling — vs aggressively — betting and raising)?

The single most profitable adjustment in low-stakes online poker: identify the calling stations and stop bluffing them. They are the majority of players at micro stakes, and they are printing money for anyone patient enough to just value-bet.

The second most profitable: identify the rocks and steal from them constantly. They fold to aggression, which means every pot they enter without a monster is free.

The two numbers that describe anyone

If you use tracking software (allowed on most sites — check yours), two numbers tell you most of what you need:

  • VPIP — “voluntarily put money in pot.” What % of hands does he choose to play? This is his looseness.
  • PFR — “preflop raise.” What % of hands does he raise with? This is his aggression.

The gap between the two numbers matters as much as the numbers. A 45/5 player calls with everything and never takes initiative — a free ATM. A 45/40 player is dangerous.

The tells that actually exist online

Physical tells are gone, but these are real and free:

Bet-size patterns. People are lazy. Watch whether someone bets 33% with draws and 75% with monsters — a huge number of amateurs do exactly this and never notice. Two hands of observation and you know their whole strategy.

Timing. An instant bet is often automatic — either a very strong hand played fast, or a routine continuation bet. A long pause followed by a call usually means a genuinely marginal hand. A long pause followed by a big raise is often genuine strength (people think before committing). Treat timing as weak evidence, not proof.

Frequency of showdowns. If someone has shown down five hands and all five were strong, they’re tight. Now you know to bluff them. This is a real read available for free after twenty minutes.

What they don’t do. He checked the flop, checked the turn, then bet the river big. Ask what hand plays that way. Usually: he missed twice and is now bluffing, or he hit the river. Two possibilities is a much better place to be than “no idea.”

The mistake with reads

Beginners find one read and marry it. “He bluffed once, so he’s a bluffer” — that’s one data point, and one data point is noise (Truth 5 again, in miniature).

Rule of thumb: change your play based on a pattern, not an incident. Three or four consistent observations, or a tracking stat over 50+ hands. Otherwise, play the standard line.

Part 10

Playing online: the practical realities

Online poker is the same game with four dials turned up. Know the dials.

Dial 1Speed

A live casino table plays ~30 hands an hour. Online, one table plays 70–100. Play four tables and you’re at 300+.

Consequence: you experience the long run much faster, which is good — your skill edge shows up sooner. But you also make mistakes 10× faster, and bad habits compound at the same rate. Play one table until you’re consistently winning. Adding tables before then just multiplies your leaks.

Dial 2No faces, more data

Covered in Part 9. The trade is: you lose physical tells (which were mostly noise) and gain exact behavioural statistics (which are signal). This is a good trade for a thinking player.

Dial 3Rake: the house’s cut, and it’s bigger than you think

The site takes a small percentage of each pot — usually around 5%, with a cap. This sounds trivial. It is not.

At the smallest stakes, a good player’s edge might be a few big blinds per 100 hands. The rake can be a comparable size. You are not just beating the other players; you’re beating the other players plus a constant tax.

Practical implications:

  • Very small pots are rake-heavy. Winning lots of tiny pots is less profitable than it looks.
  • This is another argument against playing too many hands. Every marginal hand you play is a coin flip where the house takes a cut.
  • Tournament fees work the same way — a ₹100 tournament might be ₹90 to the prize pool and ₹10 to the house.

Dial 4Table selection, which is a skill in itself

Online you can see the table list before sitting down, and this is a genuine, large, underused edge.

Look for tables with:

  • High average pot size relative to the blinds (means loose, action-heavy players)
  • High percentage of players seeing the flop (means people are playing too many hands)
  • Players you’ve already identified as calling stations

Leave tables where you’re not the best player. There is no honour in grinding out a break-even fight against a strong regular when an easier table is one click away. Choosing the right table can be worth more than a year of studying strategy.

Also: sit to the left of the aggressive players if you get the choice. That way you act after them.

The formats, briefly

Before you deposit anything

A short, unglamorous checklist:

  1. Check the legal position where you live. The rules on real-money online poker in India differ by state and have been changing; so has the tax treatment of deposits and winnings. Look up your state’s current position and the current tax rules before you put money in, rather than assuming.
  2. Play only with money whose loss would not change your month. Not “money I can afford to lose” in the abstract — money you have already mentally written off.
  3. Start at the smallest stakes available, even if they feel insultingly small. Your first thousand hands are tuition, not investment.

Part 11

The systems view: why good players still go broke

This is the part that decides whether the previous ten parts ever pay you anything. Most people who learn poker strategy still lose money, and it is almost never because their strategy was wrong.

The variance problem, stated precisely

Recall Truth 5: results are noisy. Here’s the practical shape of that noise.

Even playing well, expect:

  • Losing sessions roughly 40% of the time
  • Losing weeks regularly
  • Losing months occasionally, even as a solid winner
  • Downswings of 20+ buy-ins that feel exactly like being broken

None of that means you’re doing it wrong. It means the signal is small and the noise is large. Which creates two failure modes:

Failure mode A — going broke while being right. Your strategy is +EV, but you ran out of money before the average arrived. Solved by bankroll management.

Failure mode B — abandoning a correct strategy because it lost. You bluffed correctly, got caught three times, concluded bluffing doesn’t work, and became a calling station. Solved by judging decisions, not results.

Failure mode B is the more common and the more damaging, because it’s invisible. You don’t notice yourself getting worse.

Bankroll: your buffer against being right too early

A bankroll is money set aside only for poker. Not your rent. Not “whatever’s in my account.”

The rule is stated in buy-ins (one buy-in = a full stack at your stake, usually 100 big blinds):

Worked example. You want to play a cash game where a full stack is ₹200. At 40 buy-ins, you need a bankroll of ₹8,000, all of it dedicated to poker. If your bankroll drops to ₹5,000, you move down to a stake where a full stack is ₹125. If it grows to ₹20,000, you can consider moving up.

Why the numbers are so large: it’s not pessimism, it’s arithmetic. The swings around a small edge are big. 40 buy-ins is roughly what it takes to make the probability of going broke while being a genuine winner acceptably small.

Moving down is not failure. It’s the mechanism working. A player who moves down when the bankroll shrinks survives to play again; a player who “wins it back” at the same stake is the one who disappears.

Tilt: the feedback loop that actually kills people

Tilt is playing worse because you’re upset. It’s the single largest cost in most players’ games — larger than every strategic mistake combined.

Why it’s so dangerous: it’s self-reinforcing.

The loop has no natural exit. It has to be broken from outside, by a rule you made before you were upset.

The rules that work (set them now, while calm):

  1. Stop-loss. “If I lose 3 buy-ins in a session, I close the client.” No negotiating in the moment.
  2. Time limit. “Maximum 90 minutes.” Fatigue and tilt are close cousins.
  3. The trigger list. Write down what tips you: a specific bad beat, a specific opponent, being down for the day. When you notice one, stop. Noticing is the whole skill.
  4. Never play to get even. “I need to win it back today” is the most expensive sentence in poker. The money doesn’t know what day it is.
  5. Don’t play tired, drunk, or upset about something else. Poker is a decision-making game; you cannot make good decisions while impaired, and the table will find you.

A blunter framing: you can only lose money at poker in two ways — bad strategy, and losing control. Strategy is the part that takes months of study. Control is the part you can fix today, and it’s worth more.

The study loop

Playing more does not automatically make you better, because of Truth 5: the feedback from playing is buried in noise. You need a deliberate loop.

Three things make this loop work and are commonly skipped:

Review the hands you were unsure about, not the hands you lost. Losing a hand you played perfectly teaches you nothing. Winning a hand you played badly is the more urgent problem, and you’ll never notice it if you only review losses.

Change one thing at a time. Fix “I call too much on the river” for a week. Then fix the next thing. Changing five things at once means you learn nothing about any of them.

Ask the counterfactual, not the outcome. “Given only what I knew when I acted, what was the price and what was the chance?” That question has a right answer. “Should I have folded because he had a flush?” does not.

A short note on the harder question

Poker is a real skill game, and it is also a money game where the losing outcome is normal for most participants and the swings are engineered to feel personal. Both things are true at once.

Some honest markers that it has stopped being a game worth playing:

  • You’ve played with money set aside for something else, even once
  • You’ve hidden the amount you’re playing for from someone who’d be affected by it
  • You keep playing after your own stop-loss rule fires
  • You’re chasing losses, or thinking about the money between sessions more than the decisions
  • The sessions have stopped being enjoyable and you’re playing anyway

If several of those land, the useful move is to stop and talk to someone about it rather than to study harder — no strategy fixes that particular leak. A player who can stop is a player who can win. A player who can’t stop, can’t.

Part 12

The twelve mistakes that cost beginners everything

Ranked roughly by how much money they cost. Each one is a first-principles violation, so I’ve named which.

  1. 1

    Playing too many hands.Violates Truth 1

    The single largest leak in amateur poker. Not dramatic, just a constant drip. If you’re playing more than about 25% of hands overall, this is your biggest problem and nothing else matters until it’s fixed.

  2. 2

    Calling too much, raising too little.

    Calling is the passive option: you can only win by having the best hand. Raising can also win by making people fold. The calling player has one way to win; the raising player has two. Over thousands of hands, that difference is everything.

  3. 3

    Ignoring position.Violates Truth 3

    Playing the same hands from every seat throws away the largest free edge in the game.

  4. 4

    Not folding top pair.Reverse implied odds, Part 4

    You have a pair of kings, he’s raised you three times, and you cannot let go. “But I have top pair” is how a beginner explains losing a full stack. A strong-looking hand facing repeated aggression is often just second-best.

  5. 5

    Bluffing calling stations.Violates Part 9

    A bluff needs folds to work. Someone who never folds is immune. Betting into them with nothing is setting money on fire — and it’s the one player type where the correct play is to be less creative, not more.

  6. 6

    Chasing draws at bad prices.Violates Truth 2

    “I need one heart” is not a reason. The reason is the price. 9 outs is about 20% for one card — if you’re being asked to pay for a 40% chance, fold.

  7. 7

    Never bluffing at all.

    The opposite error, and just as costly. If you only ever bet when you have a strong hand, everyone folds when you bet and calls when you check. You’ve made yourself free to play against.

  8. 8

    Betting without a reason.Violates Part 8

    If it’s neither value nor bluff, it’s a donation.

  9. 9

    Playing scared with strong hands.

    You flop three of a kind and bet tiny because you don’t want to “scare them off.” You’ve just turned a ₹500 pot into a ₹80 pot. You cannot win big pots without building them. Bet your good hands.

  10. 10

    Reading one hand as a pattern.Violates Truth 5

    He bluffed once. That’s noise. Wait for three.

  11. 11

    Tilt.Part 11

    Bigger than 1 through 10 for many players, and the only one that can lose your entire bankroll in a single evening.

  12. 12

    Playing too high.

    Stakes chosen by ego rather than bankroll. Guarantees that a normal downswing becomes a permanent exit.

Part 13

Your first 30 days

Concrete. Follow it in order. Roughly 45–60 minutes a day.

Week 1Learn the machine (play money only)

Goal: stop needing to think about the rules at all.

  • Read Parts 0–3 of this document.
  • Play 200–300 hands of play-money 6-max. Just get fluent with the buttons and the flow.
  • Drill: hand-ranking flashcards. Deal yourself seven random cards and name your best five-card hand, out loud, in under 5 seconds. Do 30 reps a day. By day four this will be automatic, and it needs to be, because every other skill sits on top of it.
  • Drill: name the position. Every hand, before you look at your cards, say which seat you’re in and whether you act early or late.

End-of-week test: you can look at any board and any two cards and instantly say what you have, and whether you’re first or last to act.

Week 2Discipline (smallest real stakes)

Goal: fold correctly. That’s the entire week.

  • Read Parts 4–7.
  • Deposit a small, pre-written-off amount. Play at the lowest stake available with at least 40 buy-ins behind you.
  • Print the “dumb-simple” chart from Part 7 and do not deviate from it, at all, for the whole week. Not once. Even when you’re bored.
  • One table. No music, no second screen.
  • Drill: count outs on every single flop, even in hands you folded. Say the number to yourself. This is the highest-value habit in the guide and takes about a week to become automatic.

End-of-week test: you’re folding 75–80% of hands and it no longer feels strange.

Week 3Aggression and price

Goal: stop being passive; start doing arithmetic in real time.

  • Read Parts 8–9.
  • Add the continuation bet: after you raise before the flop and get one caller, bet the flop on boards with one high card and no coordination.
  • Drill: pot odds out loud. Every time you face a bet, say the two numbers before acting: “He bet 50 into 100. I need 25%. I have about 20%. Fold.” Out loud, every time. It’ll feel slow for three days and then it’ll be instant.
  • Start labelling opponents. Just two labels to begin with: “calls too much” and “folds too much.” Note them in the client’s player-notes field. Adjust: value-bet the first group, bluff the second.

End-of-week test: you can state the required percentage for any bet you face without pausing.

Week 4The loop

Goal: build the machinery that makes you better after month one.

  • Read Parts 10–12.
  • Mark 3–5 hands per session where you weren’t sure. Review them the next day, away from the table, and answer only: given what I knew, what was the price and what was the chance?
  • Write down your stop-loss and time limit and stick them where you can see them.
  • Pick one leak from Part 12 and work on only that one all week.

End-of-week test: you have a written list of your own leaks, in your own words, ranked.

After 30 days

You will not be a winning player yet — 30 days is roughly 5,000–10,000 hands, which is statistically almost nothing (Truth 5). But you will have:

  • Correct fundamentals rather than habits you’ll need to unlearn
  • A study loop that compounds
  • Rules that stop a bad night becoming a bad month

That’s the actual goal. Winning is what happens to people who have those three things and keep going.

The honest expectation to set: most people who play poker for money lose money. The ones who don’t are the ones who treat it as a discipline with homework, not as entertainment that occasionally pays. Decide which one you’re doing, and be honest about the answer.
