You already know how the pieces move. What is missing is the layer above the rules: why some moves are good, what you are supposed to be doing when nothing is obviously happening, and why a won position so often refuses to turn into a checkmate. This guide builds that layer from the ground up, starting from what the game structurally is and deriving the familiar advice (“control the centre”, “castle early”, “don’t hang pieces”) from it, so that the advice becomes something you understand rather than something you memorise.

How to use this guide

  1. 1

    Read Parts 1 and 2 once, carefully.

  2. 2

    Keep Part 5 (checkmate) and the reference card at the end open while you play for the next few weeks.

1

Part 1

The system you are in

1.1One goal; everything else is instrumental

Chess has exactly one win condition: the enemy king is attacked and has no legal way out. Nothing else scores. You get no points for material, for a beautiful attack, or for controlling the centre. Those things matter only because they make the one scoring event more likely.

That sounds too obvious to say, but it explains the two most common beginner problems in one stroke. If you attack the king directly from move five, you are skipping the steps that make mate possible. If you are a queen up and cannot finish, you have gathered the currency and do not yet know what it buys. Everything in this guide is about the pipeline between “starting position” and “no legal way out”.

1.2What kind of game this is

Three structural facts generate almost all of chess strategy.

  1. 1

    Perfect information

    First, it is a game of perfect information. Nothing is hidden. The position on the board contains the whole truth about who is better and by how much; the entire contest is about who sees more of that truth. There is no bluffing in chess, only miscalculation. This is liberating: when you lose, the reason is on the board, and it can be found and fixed.

  2. 2

    One move at a time

    Second, the players alternate, and each turn you move exactly one piece. A move is therefore a scarce resource. Any move that does not improve your position is a resource spent for nothing, and a move that forces your opponent to spend their move dealing with your threat has effectively bought you a turn. Chess players call this resource tempo.

  3. 3

    Reach depends on the piece and the square

    Third, the pieces move differently, so they control different numbers of squares, and a piece controls different numbers of squares depending on where it stands. Here is the count on an otherwise empty board:

Almost every “principle” you will ever hear is a compressed version of this table. The knight quadruples its power by moving from the rim to the centre, so “knights on the rim are dim.” The rook is the one piece whose reach does not depend on centralisation, so rooks do not care about the centre; they care about open lines, which is why you will hear “rooks belong on open files.” The queen is powerful everywhere, which is exactly why it is dangerous to expose it early: it is worth so much that any cheap piece attacking it forces it to move, and each forced move is a tempo handed to your opponent.

1.3The three currencies, and the account they all pay into

You can think of a position as a balance sheet with three currencies and one account.

Material is the pieces. A piece is a controller of squares, and its conventional value is a summary of how much it can typically do. Pawn 1, knight 3, bishop 3, rook 5, queen 9. The king has no value because losing it ends the game. These numbers are averages, not laws: a bishop staring at its own pawns is worth less than a knight sitting on a protected central square, and a rook on an open file is worth more than one buried behind its own pawns. But as a default, the table is remarkably good, and it gives you a way to evaluate any exchange: if what they lost is worth more than what you lost, the trade was good for you.

  • 1Pawn
  • 3Knight
  • 3Bishop
  • 5Rook
  • 9Queen
  • —King: no value

Time is tempo, as above. In the opening, time is spent on development: getting knights and bishops off the back rank to squares where they do something, and getting the king castled. A rule of thumb many coaches use is that three tempi are worth roughly a pawn in the opening. That is why moving the same piece twice, retreating a queen that was chased, or grabbing a pawn on the edge of the board while your pieces are still at home are all quietly expensive.

Space is the squares you control, and the centre is the most valuable space because of the table above: a piece in the centre sees more, gets to either wing faster, and denies the same squares to the enemy. Pawns on d4 and e4 (or d5 and e5 for Black) take squares away from enemy knights and bishops and give your own pieces protected posts. Space is really just future mobility: the side with more of it can bring pieces to the fight faster than the other side can bring defenders.

King safety is the account the currencies pay into. You cannot spend it, but every advantage is eventually cashed there. Its special property is that threats against a king are forcing: the opponent must respond, which hands you tempo after tempo. That is why an exposed king converts small advantages into decisive ones, and why a safe king lets you shrug off pressure that would otherwise be fatal.

1.4Why “don’t lose pieces” is very nearly the whole game at first

Material has two properties that make it dominate at the beginner level.

It compounds.

An extra piece controls extra squares, which creates extra threats, which win extra material. A one-piece lead in the middlegame tends to become a two-piece lead by the endgame without either player doing anything clever.

And it converts by recipe.

If you are a piece up and do nothing brilliant at all, you can trade the remaining pieces off, walk a pawn to the eighth rank, and deliver a textbook mate with the queen you just made. No creativity required, only technique (Part 5).

Because of these two facts, almost every game between improving players is decided by material handed over for nothing: a piece left undefended, a piece moved to a square the opponent attacks, a recapture that was never checked. Not a subtle positional squeeze; a free piece. The two skills with the highest return on effort are therefore unglamorous: stop handing material over, and take the material that is handed to you. A few hours of disciplined blunder-checking is worth more than a few hundred hours of opening study.

You mentioned trying to protect your pieces, which is the right instinct, but it needs two upgrades. The first is that protection is a count, not a feeling. A knight defended by a bishop but attacked by a pawn is still lost: the pawn takes, you recapture, and you have traded a knight for a pawn. Section 2.2 covers how to count properly. The second is that pure defence is a slow way to lose. A piece that only guards things creates no threats, and an opponent who faces no threats has all the time in the world to build threats against you. Real safety comes from activity as much as from defence: a piece that is attacking something forces the opponent to spend their moves responding, and a player who is responding is not attacking your pieces.

1.5Principles versus calculation

A principle (“develop before attacking”, “don’t move pawns in front of your castled king”) is a compressed statistic: a summary of what turned out to be true in an enormous number of positions. Calculation is looking at the concrete moves in front of you and their concrete consequences. You need both, and the skill is knowing which one is in charge at any given moment.

The rule is simple. If there is a forcing move on the board — a check, a capture, or a direct threat, for either side — calculation is in charge, because forcing moves narrow the opponent’s replies enough that you can actually see ahead. If there is no forcing move for either side, principles are in charge, and you make the move that improves your position the most. Beginners usually get this backwards: they calculate wildly in quiet positions (and drift) and play on principle in sharp ones (and get tactically punished).

1.6The win pipeline, and how to win at your level

Put the pieces together and the shape of a won game looks like this:

The chain breaks at three predictable points. It breaks at “win material” when you hand pieces away for free, so the material flows the wrong way. It breaks at “tactical chances” when you attack the king with two pieces while five sit at home, skipping the steps that create real threats. And it breaks at “textbook mate” when you get a winning position and lack the technique to close it, so the game drifts into a draw or you blunder the lead back.

So the practical answer to “how do I win?” at your current level is not “out-think the opponent”. It is: make fewer unforced errors than they do, punish theirs when they happen, and know the conversion technique cold so that a won position is actually won. As you improve, wins increasingly come from accumulating small advantages until the tactics appear on their own, but the pipeline stays the same; only the quality of each link changes.

Part 2

How to think about a single move

This is the part most beginners never get taught: a repeatable procedure to run on every move. It feels slow at first. Within a few weeks it runs in a few seconds, and it will eliminate most of your losses by itself.

2.1The routine

  1. 1

    Read their last move.

    Every move changes three things: what the moved piece now attacks, what it no longer defends, and which lines it opened or closed. Ask, in this order: what does this threaten? Is any piece of mine now attacked? Did it uncover an attack from a piece behind it? Is a check coming next move?

  2. 2

    Scan for loose pieces, both sides.

    A loose piece is one with no defender. Every loose piece on the board is a tactical target, theirs or yours. If you have one, ask whether it can be hit; if they have one, ask how to hit it.

  3. 3

    Look at your forcing moves first: checks, captures, threats, in that order.

    For each one, ask “what is their best reply?” — not the reply you hope for. Most forcing moves turn out to be bad, and that is fine; you are checking whether the position contains a tactic before you do anything quiet.

  4. 4

    If nothing forcing works, make a plan move.

    Improve your worst-placed piece, finish development, castle, put a rook on an open file, fix a weakness, or create a threat that leaves you better placed even if it is parried. Part 4 is about how to choose.

  5. 5

    Blunder-check before you touch the piece.

    Imagine the move made. Now run step 1 from their side: what can they check, capture, or threaten? Is the piece safe on the square it lands on? What did it stop guarding when it left? Is your back rank still covered? Only then move.

    The step that saves games

Step 5 is the one that saves games, and it is the one that gets skipped under excitement. The habit to build is that the moment you feel the urge to play a move is exactly the moment to pause and look for what it gives away.

Example: the knight that leaves f6

A short illustration of why step 1 comes before step 5. Suppose your opponent’s knight jumps away from f6 to attack a bishop of yours. The reflex is to move the bishop. The routine asks first what the knight stopped doing. A knight on f6 is frequently the only piece guarding h7 in front of its castled king. If you have a queen and a bishop both aimed at h7, the correct answer to “my bishop is attacked” may be to ignore it and land on h7 with check or checkmate. You will only ever see that if you read their move before you react to it.

2.2Counting: the arithmetic of “is this safe?”

Whether a piece is safe on a square is a calculation, and it is a short one.

Start with the cheapest attacker. If a piece is attacked by something worth less than itself — a knight attacked by a pawn, a rook attacked by a knight — it does not matter how many defenders it has. They take, you recapture, and you have lost the difference. Move it.

If the attackers are of equal or greater value, count attackers against defenders. More attackers than defenders means the piece falls unless you add a defender or move it. Equal numbers means you have to play the sequence out in your head: they capture with their cheapest piece, you recapture with your cheapest, they capture again, and so on, and you add up what each side lost. For example, a knight of yours defended once by a pawn and attacked twice, by a knight and a bishop: they take with the knight (you are down 3), you recapture with the pawn (their knight is gone, so the count is level), and they take your pawn with the bishop (you have lost 4 in total, they have lost 3, so you are down a pawn). Your knight was not adequately defended, even though it “had a defender.”

Two refinements. A defender that is itself pinned or overloaded is not a defender. And the count on a square changes every time a piece moves anywhere near it, which is why step 1 of the routine asks what the last move stopped defending.

2.3Time controls: play slow enough to run the routine

Fifteen minutes plus a ten-second increment, or slower

None of this can happen in a three-minute game. Until the routine is automatic, play fifteen minutes with a ten-second increment or slower, and use the time. Blitz is entertainment; it is not training, and it will actively reinforce the habit of moving before you look.

Part 3

Tactics: where material actually changes hands

3.1The geometry underneath every tactic

Tactics look like magic until you notice that they all come from a handful of geometric situations. A tactic exists when enemy pieces are aligned (two of them on one rank, file or diagonal, which invites a pin or a skewer), when they stand a knight’s fork apart (a knight attacks only squares of the opposite colour to the one it stands on, so it forks pieces standing on the same colour as each other), when one piece is doing two jobs (defending two things, or defending one thing while blocking a line), when a piece is loose (undefended, so any attack on it is a real threat), when the king has few escape squares, or when a piece has few safe squares and can be trapped.

That gives you a scanning list. Instead of “is there a tactic here?”, which is unanswerable, ask the concrete questions:

  • Is anything loose?
  • Are two of their pieces on a line?
  • Is one of their pieces defending two things?
  • Is their king short of squares?
  • Is any piece of theirs short of squares?

Each “yes” is a candidate for a forcing move, which sends you back to step 3 of the routine.

3.2The motifs

Do not memorise the table. Do puzzles, and the shapes will become recognisable the way faces are recognisable, without you naming the features.

3.3Calculating forcing sequences

Forcing moves are calculable because they restrict the reply. A check that can be answered only by a king move is a move you can see through completely. A capture that must be recaptured is almost as good. Combinations are simply chains of these: check, check, capture, and the material or the mate falls out at the end. When you calculate, look at the most forcing move first at every branch, because it has the fewest replies to consider, and only widen the search if the forcing lines come to nothing.

The in-between move

One useful habit inside a sequence: when you are “supposed” to recapture, pause and ask whether there is a stronger forcing move first. The expected recapture will often still be there a move later; a check or a bigger threat played in between (players call it an in-between move) frequently wins more.

3.4Defence uses the same tools

When a piece of yours is attacked, you have five kinds of answers, and it is worth running through them in order rather than grabbing the first:

  1. 1Move it
  2. 2Capture the attacker
  3. 3Block the attack
  4. 4Add a defender
  5. 5Make a bigger threat somewhere else and let them deal with that instead

The last one is the most underused at your level and the most powerful; it is how you turn a defensive moment into tempo.

What does my opponent want?

Better still is the question that prevents most emergencies before they exist: after each of their moves, ask “what does my opponent want?” If you can see their plan two moves before it arrives, you can usually spoil it for free.

Part 4

Strategy: what to do when nothing is forcing

Each phase of the game has a different dominant logic, and a lot of beginner confusion comes from applying the wrong phase’s logic.

  • OpeningMobilise
  • MiddlegameRead the imbalances, then improve your worst piece
  • EndgameThe king becomes a piece

4.1The opening: mobilise

The opening’s job is to get your army into the game and your king out of it. Concretely: put one or two pawns in the centre, bring the knights and bishops off the back rank to squares where they control central squares, castle, and connect your rooks (so they defend each other along the back rank). If you have done all of that by about move ten and lost nothing on the way, your opening was a success, whatever the “theory” says.

  1. Centre pawns
  2. Knights and bishops out
  3. Castle
  4. Connect the rooks
by about move ten

The standard opening advice follows directly from the currency model.

  • Do not move the same piece twice without a reason, because each repeat is a tempo not spent developing.

  • Do not bring the queen out early, because every cheap piece that attacks it gains your opponent a developing move with tempo.

  • Do not grab pawns on the edge of the board while your pieces are at home, because the tempi spent going there and coming back are usually worth more than the pawn, and the piece that went is often trapped.

  • Do not make many pawn moves, because pawn moves do not develop pieces and cannot be undone.

  • And castle early, because the middlegame is about to open lines, and a king in the middle of open lines is what every tactic in Part 3 feeds on.

Knights usually come out before bishops, for a practical reason: a knight has only two or three sensible squares in the opening, while a bishop’s best diagonal depends on where the pawns end up, so committing the knight first keeps your options open.

You do not need opening theory yet. You need one setup per colour that you can reach against almost anything, so that your opening moves are automatic and your thinking time is saved for the middlegame.

As White

Play e4: the Italian shape

  1. e4
  2. Nf3
  3. Bc4
  4. d3
  5. castle

As White

Or play d4: the London shape

  1. d4
  2. Nf3
  3. Bf4
  4. e3
  5. Bd3
  6. c3
  7. castle

As Black

Against e4, play e5

  1. e5
  2. Nc6
  3. Nf6
  4. Bc5orBe7
  5. castle

As Black

Against d4, play d5

  1. d5
  2. Nf6
  3. e6
  4. Be7
  5. castle

Every one of those systems puts a pawn in the centre, brings the minor pieces to sensible squares, and castles by about move seven. When your opponent does something strange, do not try to punish it; keep developing and castle, and the strangeness usually punishes itself.

One trap to know because it is aimed at you

The pawn on f7 (f2 for White) is defended by nothing but the king at the start of the game. Early queen sorties (queen to h5 followed by bishop to c4, both aiming at f7) target it. The correct reaction is not panic but development: block with a pawn or knight where needed and attack the queen with developing moves, so that every move the queen makes costs your opponent a tempo.

4.2The middlegame: read the imbalances, then improve your worst piece

The middlegame is where players who “know the rules” feel lost, because there is no forcing move, no development left to do, and no obvious target. The way out is a single question that always has an answer:

The question that always has an answerWhich of my pieces is doing the least, and where would it do more?

Move that piece toward that square. Then ask again. Grandmasters describe most of their quiet moves exactly this way.

To answer it well, learn to read the position’s imbalances, the ways in which the two sides differ. Material is one, and you already know how to count it. Pawn structure is the terrain: which pawns are weak (isolated, with no neighbour to defend them; doubled on the same file; or left behind by their neighbours), which files have no pawns on them and are therefore highways for rooks, and where the pawn chains point, which is the direction each side should attack. Piece activity is the next: a bishop on a long open diagonal is worth more than one blocked by its own pawns; a knight on a protected central square that no enemy pawn can ever attack (an outpost) is a monster; a rook on an open file is doing its job and a rook behind its own pawns is not. King safety and space round it out.

Plans follow from imbalances almost mechanically.

Because you can see these things by looking, the middlegame stops being a fog and becomes a set of small, checkable questions.

Where the pieces want to be, as defaults:

Two things about pawns

Two things about pawns deserve emphasis because they are invisible to most beginners. Pawns cannot move backwards, so every pawn move is a permanent decision; the squares a pawn leaves behind are never guarded by it again. And the pawns in front of your castled king are the walls of its house. Moving them “to kick a piece” or “to gain space” opens the door for every attack that follows.

4.3The endgame: the king becomes a piece

When most of the pieces are gone, the logic flips. The king, which spent the game hiding, becomes one of your strongest pieces (it controls eight squares, remember) and should walk to the centre. Pawns, which were terrain, become the objective, because a pawn that reaches the eighth rank becomes a queen and the game is over.

A handful of ideas carry almost all endgame play at your level.

The passed pawn

A passed pawn (no enemy pawn ahead of it on its own file or the two neighbouring files) is the most valuable thing on the board; create one, protect it, push it.

Rooks behind passed pawns

Rooks belong behind passed pawns, yours or theirs, because from behind they support every advance without ever needing to move.

Opposition

Opposition is when the two kings stand on the same line with one square between them, and the side that does not have to move has it; it decides most king-and-pawn endings, because the king with the opposition can force the other to step aside.

The king leads the pawn

And the king must lead the pawn, not follow it: a king in front of its own pawn usually wins, a king behind it usually only draws.

Endgame technique also has two failure modes that turn wins into draws, and both come up again in Part 5:

Stalemate

The opponent’s king has no legal move but is not in check.

The fifty-move rule

Fifty consecutive moves by each side pass without a capture or pawn move.

Both are the result of shuffling instead of executing a known procedure.

4.4When to trade

Trading is not neutral; every exchange changes who benefits, and this is one of the places where thinking in currencies pays off directly.

Ahead in material

Trade pieces but not pawns

Each trade makes your extra material a larger share of what remains and removes a defender that could have stopped your eventual promotion; your pawns are your future queens, so keep them.

Behind

Do the opposite

Avoid trades, keep the position complicated, and give your opponent chances to go wrong.

Cramped

Trade

The cramped side wants trades.

More space

Avoid trades

The side with more space avoids them.

Whenever they let you

Trade your worst piece for their best

Queenless endgame, extra pawn

Keep trading pieces

Keep going until it is king and pawns against king and pawns; that ending you will learn to win by technique.

Part 5

Checkmate: the geometry, the technique, the patterns

This is the part you asked about most directly, so it gets the most space.

Reading the diagrams a square the king can still reach an escape square your pieces cover the king is in check

5.1Why checkmate is hard: the arithmetic of escape squares

Checkmate has four conditions, all of which must hold at once:

  • The king is in check.
  • Every square it could step to is either occupied by its own pieces or attacked by yours.
  • The checking piece cannot be captured.
  • The check cannot be blocked.

Beginners think of mate as “give check and hope.” Mate is actually a coverage problem, and the numbers explain why it feels so hard.

A king in the open has eight neighbouring squares. To mate it there you must control all eight and give a safe check, which takes an enormous amount of force. A king on the edge has five neighbours. A king in the corner has three. Its own pieces reduce the count further: a castled king behind three unmoved pawns has exactly two free squares on the back rank, and if a rook stands next to it, one.

This is the whole secret of checkmate in one sentence:

Drive the king to the edge, let its own pieces be the walls, and only then give check.

It also explains why a lone queen can never mate a lone king without help. A queen adjacent to the king covers most of its squares but can simply be captured; a queen at a distance cannot cover them all. Mate is a team effort, and in the simplest endgames the second team member is your own king.

5.2Restrict first, check last

The most common beginner failure is check-chasing: giving every check available, one after another, because check feels like progress. It usually is not. A check the king can answer by stepping toward the centre has improved the enemy king’s position and cost you a tempo. The game goes on for forty moves, the fifty-move rule or a stalemate arrives, and a queen-up position ends in a draw.

Replace the reflex with a rule.

Give a check only if it does one of three things:

Delivers mate
Wins material
Forces the king toward the edge or corner

Otherwise, take squares away with quiet moves.

Before any check, count the squares the king will be able to step to afterwards; if the number is not zero and the check gains nothing else, do not play it.

5.3Convert boring

When you are far ahead, you do not need a brilliant mate. You need a boring one. Trade the remaining pieces (not the pawns), push a pawn to the eighth rank, and mate with the resulting queen using the technique below. Resist the urge to hunt the king through the middle of the board with three pieces while your opponent still has a rook; that is how leads get thrown away.

Two things can spoil a won position and both are avoidable.

  • Stalemate: when the opponent has almost nothing left, check on every move whether the move you are about to play leaves them any legal move at all; if it does not and you are not giving check, it is a draw.
  • The fifty-move rule: if you have been shuffling for a while without a plan, stop and execute one of the procedures below instead.

5.4The three mates to drill until they are automatic

Under a minute each, without thinking

Practise each of these against a computer until you can finish it in under a minute without thinking. This is the single most direct fix for “I get winning positions and cannot finish.”

The two-rook ladder

Put your rooks on adjacent ranks (or files). One rook gives check along its rank; the king cannot step toward the other rook’s rank, so it must retreat one rank toward the edge. Now the other rook checks on the next rank. Alternate, and the king is walked backwards like a ladder until it stands on the edge and the final check is mate. If the king steps toward a rook to attack it, slide that rook to the far side of the board along the same rank; it still does its job. Keep your own king out of the way.

King and queen against king

The queen’s box method has four steps, and the third one is where games are drawn.

  1. 1

    Step one

    Place the queen a knight’s move away from the enemy king, on the side you want to push it away from. From that distance it is never adjacent, so it can never be captured, and it takes away the whole file and the whole rank next to the king on your side, plus two more squares on the diagonals. In the diagram below the black king has only three legal moves, all of them away from the queen.

  2. 2

    Step two

    Whenever the king moves, move the queen so that it is again a knight’s move away, on the same side. The king’s box shrinks, without a single check being given.

  3. 3

    Step three

    When the king reaches the edge, fence it there and stop. Put the queen on the rank (or file) directly in front of the king, well away from it, so that the king is confined to the edge but still has a square or two to shuffle on. Then do not touch the queen again until your king has arrived. Every “one more shrinking move” against a king on the edge risks the stalemate below.

  4. 4

    Step four

    Walk your own king up until it protects the square directly in front of the enemy king, either from directly opposite with one square between the kings, or from diagonally next to that square. Then mate: the queen lands on the square in front of the enemy king, protected by your king, or it checks along the edge from a distance while your king covers the squares in front.

  5. The trap

    And here is the trap. Black’s king is in the corner; White’s queen has followed it one step too far, to the knight’s-move square where step two would have put it; White’s king is far away. Black is not in check and has no legal move. It is a draw, from a position that is trivially won with the king brought up first.

King and rook against king

A single rook cannot shrink a box the way a queen can, so the king has to do more of the work.

  1. 1

    Step one: fence

    Put the rook on a rank that cuts the enemy king off from the centre, so it lives in a strip on the far side.

  2. 2

    Step two

    Walk your king up to the fence, toward theirs.

  3. 3

    Step three: opposition

    Whenever the two kings stand on the same file with one square between them, a rook check on the enemy king’s rank drives it back one rank, because your king covers the squares in front of it and the rook covers its own rank; the rook’s new rank is the new fence. Repeat, and the king reaches the edge, where the same check is mate because there is nothing left to retreat to.

  4. 4

    Step four: the waiting move

    Step four is the trick, because the enemy king will not step into opposition voluntarily; it sidesteps. When its king is one file to the side of yours and it is your move, do not chase it: make a waiting move with the rook along its rank, keeping the fence and staying well out of the king’s reach. Now the king must move, and every choice helps you: step into opposition, and the rook check drives it back (or mates, on the edge); step away along the rank, and you follow with your king; step backwards, and it has retreated on its own. Sooner or later it runs out of board and has to stand in front of you. If the enemy king ever attacks the rook, move the rook to the far end of the same rank; it stays a fence and gets out of reach.

5.5Mating patterns that appear in real middlegames

These are the same geometry with the opponent’s own pieces as the walls. Learn to see the shapes; they come up constantly at every level.

The back rank

A castled king with its three pawns unmoved has two free squares on its rank. If nothing of the opponent’s guards that rank and a rook or queen can reach it safely, the pawns that were supposed to protect the king become its coffin. Before you move your last rook off your own back rank, check for this against yourself, and if it is a danger, make a pawn move in front of your king (h3 or g3 for White) to give it an escape hatch.

Checkmate

The smothered mate

A knight’s check cannot be blocked, and a king completely surrounded by its own pieces cannot step away. The famous version begins with a queen sacrifice on g8 that drags the rook into the corner beside the king; the knight then does the rest.

Checkmate

Queen next to the king, protected by anything

The single most common mate in real games. A queen adjacent to the enemy king covers almost all of its squares; it only needs one protector so that the king cannot take it, and the king’s own pawns do the rest. The protector can be a knight, a bishop, a pawn or a rook. Whenever your queen can reach a square next to the enemy king that one of your other pieces also controls, stop and count the escape squares.

The knight on f5 protects the queen on g7; the pawns on f7 and h7 block the king’s escape; the queen covers f8 and h8. The rook on a8 is a spectator.

Checkmate

The general lesson from all threeThe enemy king’s own pieces are its prison; you only need to supply the roof and the check.

5.6Attacking the king while there are still pieces on the board

Sometimes you should attack the king directly in the middlegame, and this is how to tell.

  • Count the pieces you can bring to bear on the king’s position against the pieces that defend it; an attack with fewer attackers than defenders does not work, however exciting it looks.
  • Look for open lines toward the king: a file with no pawns on it, a diagonal that a bishop can occupy, a pawn in front of the king that has already moved.
  • Count the king’s escape squares before you begin, and ask which of your pieces can take away the ones that are still free.

Sacrifice only when you can calculate the consequences to the end, to mate or to a clear material gain. “It looks dangerous” is not a calculation. And if the attack does not work, do not launch it anyway; go back to Part 4, improve your worst piece, and win material instead. An attack that fails leaves your pieces scattered and your opponent’s centralised, which is precisely the position from which they start winning material.

A practice plan for the next six weeks

The improvement loop is short: play slow games with the routine, find the first mistake in each loss, fix the category of mistake, repeat. Openings can wait; almost nothing you lose right now is lost in the opening.

  2. 2Find the first mistake in each loss

  4. Repeat

Every day

Tactics puzzles

15 min

Every day, spend about fifteen minutes on tactics puzzles (the puzzle trainers on Lichess, which is free, or Chess.com are rated, so they adapt to your level). Puzzles are how the shapes in Part 3 become recognisable without conscious effort. Do them slowly and try to see the whole sequence before you move; guessing teaches you to guess.

Lichess puzzles Chess.com puzzles

Slow games, then one review

1–2 games, 15 + 10

Play one or two games a day at fifteen minutes plus ten seconds or slower, and run the routine in Part 2 on every single move, even the obvious ones. When a game ends, do not start another one immediately. Open the analysis and find the first move on which material changed hands by mistake, yours or theirs. Ask which step of the routine would have caught it, and write it down as a category:

  • did not read their last move
  • moved a piece to an attacked square
  • left a piece undefended
  • miscounted an exchange
  • check-chased
  • stalemated

After two weeks you will find that two or three categories account for most of your losses, and those become the only things you need to fix.

The three mates

5 min

Spend five minutes a day on the three mates in Section 5.4 against a computer, from random positions, until each takes you under a minute. Once that is true, add king and pawn against king (the opposition idea from Section 4.3) and start converting extra-pawn endgames the same way.

Reference card

Every move

  1. What did their last move attack, stop defending, or uncover? Is a check coming?
  2. Any loose pieces, theirs or mine?
  3. My checks, captures, threats, in that order. Their best reply to each.
  4. Nothing forcing: improve the worst piece, develop, castle, rook to an open file.
  5. Blunder-check: after my move, what can they check, capture or threaten? Is the piece safe where it lands? What did it stop guarding?

Counting

Attacked by something cheaper: move, whatever the defenders. Otherwise count attackers against defenders and play the exchange out in your head.

Opening

Centre pawn, knights, bishops, castle, connect rooks, by move ten. Same piece twice, early queen, edge pawn grabs and extra pawn moves all cost tempo.

Trades

Ahead: trade pieces, keep pawns. Behind: avoid trades, complicate. Cramped: trade. More space: don’t.

Checkmate

Drive the king to the edge; count its escape squares; check only when the check mates, wins material or pushes it toward the edge. Mate is a team effort: your king is a piece. When far ahead, trade down, promote, and use the queen technique. Against a bare king on the edge: stop the queen, bring the king, watch for stalemate.

Endgame

Centralise the king. Create a passed pawn and push it. Rooks behind passed pawns. King leads the pawn. Learn opposition.

How you win at this level

Fewer unforced errors than your opponent, punish theirs, and finish with technique.
