Game theory from first principles

Thinking in Games

Game theory studies decisions that depend on other people's decisions. This guide builds it from the ground up: what it is, where it hides in daily life, what happens when the prisoner's dilemma has a thousand prisoners, and how to use it to think more clearly.

About 30 minutes Five interactive models

The short version

Four questions, four answers

What is it?

The study of decisions whose outcome depends on other people's decisions. Its central idea is the equilibrium: a set of moves where each person's choice is the best reply to everyone else's. An equilibrium is stable. It can still be bad for everyone.

Parts 1 to 4 How does it show up in life?

Anywhere more than one mind shapes the outcome: traffic, prices, salaries, meetings, marketplaces, families, even your present self against your future self. Most of these games repeat, and most are positive-sum. Both facts change the smart move.

Part 5 What happens with many players?

The dilemma gets harsher. Your contribution becomes invisible while your saving from skipping it stays whole. Defectors hide in the crowd, and the only way to retaliate also hurts cooperators. Groups that cooperate at scale do it with structure: small units, visible contributions, named owners, graduated sanctions.

Part 6 How do I think this way?

Name the game before you play it. Ask whether cooperation is failing from greed or from fear. Treat most relationships as repeated. When a game is bad, change the game instead of trying to play it better.

Parts 7 and 8

Part 1

What game theory is

Start with one decision

Picture yourself choosing a route home at 3 a.m. The roads are empty. You pick the fastest route and you are done. The world does not react to your choice. That is a plain decision problem.

Now make it 6 p.m. The fastest route depends on which routes everyone else takes. Their choices depend on what they expect people like you to do. "Best route" has no answer until you have a belief about the others, and they need a belief about you. I think that you think that I think, without end.

Game theory cuts this loop. John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern set out the field in their 1944 book. John Nash gave it its central idea in 1950. It answers one question precisely: what should you do when your outcome depends on the choices of others who are reasoning about you?

"Game" covers any situation with this structure: a price war, a salary negotiation, two cars at a junction, a vaccination drive, a family deciding who washes the dishes. "Strategic" means taking into account that the others are thinking too.

The five building blocks

Every game, from chess to a climate treaty, is defined by five things. Change any one of them and you have a different game.

Players

Who decides. Include the ones outside the room.

Actions

What each player can do, including waiting, walking away or doing nothing. A strategy is a complete plan for every situation that could come up.

Payoffs

What each player values. Time, status, fairness, sleep and self-image count, along with money.

Information

Who knows what, and when. Can you see what others did? Do they know what you know?

Timing

At the same time, like sealed bids, or in turns, like chess? Played once or again and again?

Behaviour that looks irrational is usually rational play of a game whose payoffs you have misread.

Equilibrium

Three ideas do most of the work.

  • Best response: your best move, given what the others are doing.
  • Dominant strategy: a move that is your best response to everything the others might do. If you have one, play it. You do not even need to predict the others.
  • Nash equilibrium: a set of moves, one per player, where each move is a best response to the others. Nobody can do better by changing only their own move.

An equilibrium is stable, and stability says nothing about quality. It can be terrible for everyone and still persist, because no single person can fix it alone.

Many social problems are equilibria. That is why speeches rarely fix them.

A game can also have several equilibria. Driving on the left and driving on the right are both equilibria. History and expectations decide which one a society lands in, and so do obvious meeting points. Thomas Schelling called these focal points. He asked people where they would meet a stranger in New York with no way to communicate. Most picked Grand Central Station, and almost all picked noon.

The other benchmark is Pareto efficiency: an outcome where nobody can be made better off without making someone worse off. Much of game theory studies the gap between the two. Equilibrium describes what individuals will do. Efficiency describes what would be best for the group.

Two more tools

Look forward, reason backward. When players move in turns, solve the game from the end. Ask what each player will do at the last step, then work back to the first. This is how you spot an empty threat. If carrying out a threat would hurt the person making it, they will back off when the moment comes, and a thinking opponent ignores it.

Sometimes the best move is to be unpredictable. When the other side gains by predicting you, mixing your moves at random is rational. A penalty taker who always shoots left gets stopped. Ignacio Palacios-Huerta studied penalty kicks in professional European football and found that players mixed their shots the way the theory predicts. Tax audits, security checks and a bowler's variations work the same way.

Part 2

Greed and fear: the family of two-player games

Give two players two choices: cooperate or defect. There are four outcomes, each with a standard name.

RRewardYou both cooperate.
TTemptationYou defect while the other cooperates.
SSucker's payoffYou cooperate while the other defects.
PPunishmentYou both defect.

The character of the game comes down to two comparisons. The sociologists Michael Macy and Andreas Flache named them greed and fear.

Greed T > R

Exploiting a cooperator pays more than cooperating with them.

Fear S < P

Being the lone cooperator is worse than defecting together.

Two yes-or-no questions give four games. Drag the point to move between them.

Fear, no greed

Stag Hunt

From Rousseau. Two hunters can catch a stag together or a hare each alone. The stag is better, but only if the other hunter shows up.

Seen in: a team adopting a new tool, friends moving to a new messaging app, a team agreeing to keep Fridays free of meetings.

What helps: assurance. Go first with something small. Offer a guarantee, a pilot or a money-back promise. Make your commitment conditional: "I'm in if you're in."

Greed and fear

Prisoner's Dilemma

Both problems at once. Each player does better by defecting whatever the other does.

Seen in: two firms choosing advertising budgets, two countries building weapons, two sellers deciding whether to buy fake reviews.

What helps: changing the payoffs or the time horizon. Contracts, monitoring, repetition and reputation.

No greed, no fear

Harmony

Cooperating is best whatever the other does. The interests line up.

Seen in: co-authors who both want the paper to be good.

What helps: keeping it that way. Controls added here signal distrust and can create the fear they guard against.

Greed, no fear

Chicken

Two cars race toward each other. The first to swerve loses face. If neither swerves, they crash. Biologists call it Hawk–Dove.

Seen in: an unsignalled junction, a negotiation near its deadline, who apologises first after a fight.

What helps: conventions and commitment. Right-of-way rules decide who yields. The driver who visibly throws away the steering wheel wins, provided the other driver sees it.

A common mistake is to treat a fear problem as a greed problem. Adding surveillance to a team that is afraid of being let down signals distrust, and the fear grows.

Part 3

The prisoner's dilemma, closely

In 1950, Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher at the RAND Corporation ran an experiment on a game whose equilibrium was plainly bad. Albert Tucker gave it a story. Two suspects are held in separate rooms. If both stay silent, both get a short sentence. If one confesses and the other stays silent, the confessor goes free and the silent one gets a long sentence. If both confess, both get a medium sentence.

In points, with higher meaning better (the numbers Robert Axelrod used):

They cooperateThey defect You cooperate3, 30, 5 You defect5, 01, 1

Work through it from your side. If they cooperate, defecting gets you 5 instead of 3. If they defect, defecting gets you 1 instead of 0. Defecting is better whatever they do, so it is a dominant strategy. They reason the same way. You both defect and get 1 each, when 3 each was on the table.

Nobody in this story is stupid or cruel. Each player makes the individually correct choice, and the structure turns those correct choices into a bad result. This is the most useful idea in the field.

Good people inside a bad structure produce bad outcomes. To change the outcome, change the structure: the payoffs, the information, the time horizon. Lecturing the players does little.

For the curiousA game is a prisoner's dilemma when T > R > P > S. Researchers usually add 2R > T + S, so that taking turns exploiting each other is worse than steady cooperation.

Part 4

When the game repeats

The one-shot prisoner's dilemma is a special case. Most of life repeats: the same colleagues, suppliers, neighbours and family. When you will meet again, today's move shapes tomorrow's treatment, and the game changes.

In 1980 the political scientist Robert Axelrod invited experts to submit computer programs to play a repeated prisoner's dilemma against each other, 200 rounds per match. Fourteen programs entered. The shortest one won: Tit for Tat, sent in by the psychologist Anatol Rapoport. It cooperates on the first move and then copies whatever the other player did last. Axelrod published the results and ran a second tournament. Sixty-two programs entered, many written to beat Tit for Tat. It won again.

What the winners had in common

Don't be the first to defect

Every top strategy was nice: it never defected first.

Reciprocate cooperation and defection

Punish defection at once, so nobody can exploit you. Return to cooperation as soon as the other does.

Don't be envious

Tit for Tat never outscores the player it faces. It wins by drawing cooperation out of others. In a positive-sum game, doing well matters more than beating the person in front of you.

Don't be too clever

Simple, readable strategies let others learn quickly that cooperating with you pays.

The shadow of the future

Cooperation lasts only while the future matters enough. If both players know the exact last round, cooperation unravels backward: defect in the last round, so defect in the one before, and so on. In the lab, people cooperate until near the end and then defect. Relationships with a visible end date are where cooperation is weakest: the last deal with a vendor, the notice period, the final months of a manager's tenure.

For the curiousWith the standard payoffs, Tit for Tat is stable against every alternative only if the chance of meeting again, w, is at least 2/3. The condition is w ≥ max[(T − R)/(T − P), (T − R)/(R − S)]

Mistakes change the answer

Real interactions are noisy. A late reply reads as a snub. An honest error reads as betrayal. Tit for Tat handles noise badly: one accidental defection starts an echo of retaliation that can run for a long time. Strategies that forgive do better. Generous Tit for Tat (Martin Nowak and Karl Sigmund, 1992) forgives a defection about one time in three with the standard payoffs. Pavlov repeats its last move after a good result and switches after a bad one. In a noisy world, be slightly more forgiving than strict reciprocity demands.

Winners multiply

Axelrod also ran ecological versions in which successful strategies grew in number each generation. Exploiters did well at first, feeding on naive cooperators. As the naive disappeared, the exploiters starved, and reciprocating strategies took over. Try it below: shorten the relationship, add mistakes, and watch who survives.

Nature runs the same experiment. A vampire bat that fails to find blood is often fed by a roost-mate, and bats give more to those that gave to them (Gerald Wilkinson, 1984; Gerald Carter and Wilkinson, 2013).

Everyday life
  • At a concert, one person stands for a better view. The person behind stands too. Soon everyone is standing and nobody sees better than when all were seated.
  • A party called for 7 starts at 9. Once everyone expects lateness, arriving on time is a losing move. One punctual guest cannot move the equilibrium. Only a shared change of expectations can.
  • At an unsignalled junction, every car noses forward to claim the gap. Between two cars it is chicken. Among twenty, it is gridlock: each car that enters the box gains a little, and together they block everyone.
  • Once everyone honks, a honk conveys nothing, and you still have to honk to be noticed.
Work
  • In a meeting, everyone waits for someone else to ask the obvious question. Asking it in the room does more than asking in a DM, because the room turns private doubts into common knowledge.
  • A shared codebase is a commons. Each shortcut is sensible for the person taking it. Technical debt is the sum.
  • If next year's target is set from this year's result, beating the target raises the bar, and sandbagging becomes rational. Economists call this the ratchet effect (Martin Weitzman, 1980).
  • A promotion that goes to the best of a group is a tournament (Edward Lazear and Sherwin Rosen, 1981). It motivates effort and discourages helping peers.
  • When a measure becomes a target, the people being measured start playing against the measurer. Goodhart's law describes a game.
Markets and platforms
  • Price wars and advertising wars are prisoner's dilemmas between firms. Quiet cooperation between rivals is repeated-game cooperation, which is why competition law exists.
  • Buyers cannot check quality before they buy. George Akerlof showed in 1970 that hidden quality can drive good products out of a market. Marketplaces answer with ratings, return policies, seller guarantees and evidence at handover, such as photos at pickup and delivery. Each one makes a hidden action visible, and a policy change on a large platform rewrites the payoffs of millions of players at once.
  • A long warranty, free returns, or a founder putting in their own money are believable because they would be expensive if the claim behind them were false. Michael Spence called these costly signals (1973).
  • In a second-price auction, the highest bidder wins and pays the second-highest bid. Bidding your true value becomes the best strategy (William Vickrey, 1961). The rules make honesty the smart play.
Home and relationships
  • Chores in a shared home form a public-goods game. Dishes pile up because each extra dish feels like someone else's problem.
  • Who apologises first after a fight is a game of chicken. A small ritual that makes yielding cheap helps more than winning the standoff.
  • Long relationships are repeated games with noise. Forgiving the occasional lapse beats strict tit for tat.
Society and nature
  • In a city's winter smog, every vehicle, construction site and crop fire adds a sliver. No sliver decides the outcome. Everyone breathes the total.
  • When a water table falls, households drill deeper borewells, which lowers the water table further for everyone.
  • Each unvaccinated person free-rides on everyone else's immunity. Each unnecessary course of antibiotics spends down a shared resource.
  • Many animal contests are ritual displays, because escalating hurts both sides (John Maynard Smith and George Price, 1973).
Inside your own head
  • Your present self and your future self are two players with different payoffs. Procrastination is your present self defecting on your future self.
  • Commitment devices work by changing the payoffs, like Ulysses tied to the mast.
  • Personal rules work because they turn each temptation into a repeated game. The psychiatrist George Ainslie pointed out that breaking a rule once predicts breaking it again, so each lapse costs more than the moment.

Part 6

Beyond two players

Nash's 1950 paper was already titled Equilibrium Points in N-Person Games. The logic carries over to any number of players. The consequences get sharper.

The same dilemma with ten people

Ten people each hold ₹100. Each can put any part of it into a common pot. The pot is tripled and shared equally among all ten, whether or not they put anything in.

What happensThe pot becomesYou end withEach contributor ends with
Everyone puts in everything₹3,000₹300₹300
Nobody puts anything in₹0₹100No contributors
The other nine put in everything; you keep yours₹2,700₹370₹270
You alone put in everything₹300₹30₹30

Every rupee you put in creates ₹3 for the group and returns 30 paise to you. Keeping your money is a dominant strategy. Yet everyone contributing (₹300 each) beats everyone keeping (₹100 each). This is the public-goods game: the prisoner's dilemma with many players. The key number is your private return on each rupee, the multiplier divided by the group size (3 ÷ 10 here). While it is below 1, contributing costs you personally even though it creates value overall.

Four things change as the group grows

Your impact dilutes, your temptation stays whole

With two players, your choice decides half the outcome. With a thousand, your contribution disappears in the total, while the saving from skipping it is entirely yours.

Defection hides

With two players, you know who defected. With a thousand, defectors are anonymous, and reputation, the engine of repeated-game cooperation, stalls.

Retaliation hits the innocent

In a group, you can only retaliate by withholding your own contribution, which punishes cooperators too. Cooperators who feel cheated pull back. That cheats other cooperators, who pull back as well.

Enforcement is itself a public good

Somebody has to monitor and punish free riders. That costs the enforcer and benefits everyone. This second-order free-rider problem is why cooperation at scale needs institutions as well as goodwill.

Mancur Olson made the political version of this argument in 1965. A small industry that gains a lot from a tariff organises easily. Millions of consumers who each lose a little never organise. Small, concentrated interests often beat large, diffuse majorities.

Size alone does not decide the outcome. In experiments that held the private return fixed, groups of 40 and 100 contributed more efficiently than groups of 4 and 10 (Mark Isaac, James Walker and Arlington Williams, 1994). The four forces above cause the trouble, and each of them can be designed against.

What experiments show

In laboratory public-goods games, people start out generous. First-round contributions usually run between 40 and 60 per cent of what people hold. Round by round, contributions decline. In one of Ernst Fehr and Simon Gächter's treatments without punishment, contributions fell to about 10 per cent by the final round.

Most people in these games are willing to cooperate. In one study, about half the participants were conditional cooperators, who gave more when others gave more. About a third were free riders (Urs Fischbacher, Gächter and Fehr, 2001). Conditional cooperators match others, slightly below. Put them in a group with a few free riders and each round's contributions drop a little below the last.

Targeted punishment changes the picture. When participants could pay to fine specific low contributors, contributions rose and stayed high, even though punishing cost the punisher money and earned them nothing back (Fehr and Gächter, 2000). In fixed groups with punishment, contributions reached about 90 per cent of the maximum by the end.

Punishment has limits. Benedikt Herrmann, Christian Thöni and Gächter ran the experiment in 16 cities around the world (2008). In some of them, free riders punished the cooperators. This antisocial punishment was more common where norms of civic cooperation and the rule of law were weaker, and where it was common, the chance to punish failed to raise cooperation. Sanctions work when the surrounding norms support them.

A picture for many players: the Schelling diagram

Thomas Schelling drew a picture that works whenever many people make the same yes-or-no choice (1973). Along the bottom, put the share of other people who cooperate. Draw two lines: your payoff if you cooperate and your payoff if you defect. Where the cooperate line is higher, people drift toward cooperating. Where the defect line is higher, they drift toward defecting. The points where the drift stops are the equilibria.

Interventions move the lines. A flat fine for defecting lowers the defect line. Make it big enough and cooperating becomes the best move at every level. Social pressure works differently. Defecting when most people cooperate draws disapproval. Defecting when nobody cooperates draws none. That kind of pressure tilts the defect line, so the two lines cross. The crossing is a tipping point. Above it, cooperation sustains itself. Below it, cooperation collapses. Norms behave this way. In Robert Cialdini's experiments, people littered more in a place that was already littered than in a clean one (1990).

Schelling's own example was ice hockey. Players chose to play without helmets, for better vision and to look tough, yet many said they would welcome a rule requiring helmets. A rule that binds everyone at once can be welcomed by the very people who would never act alone. People sometimes ask to be regulated.

Some shared things become more valuable as more people use them: roads, markets, festivals, languages. The legal scholar Carol Rose called this the comedy of the commons (1986). Network effects are its modern form.

"Someone should": the volunteer's dilemma

Some goods need only one person to act. Someone has to call the ambulance, fix the broken build, or ask the awkward question. Acting costs the actor a little. Everyone benefits if anyone acts. Each person would prefer someone else to do it.

Andreas Diekmann modelled this in 1985. In the game's equilibrium, each person's chance of acting falls steeply as the group grows, while the expected number of volunteers stays almost flat. Adding people divides the help without adding to it.

The practical lesson holds. The bigger the audience, the less any one person feels the job is theirs. First-aid courses teach you to point at one bystander and say "You, call an ambulance." At work, the equivalent is a named owner for every shared problem.

Tipping points: Granovetter's crowd

The sociologist Mark Granovetter imagined 100 people in a square, each deciding whether to join a riot (1978). Each has a threshold: the number of others who must join first. Suppose the thresholds run 0, 1, 2 and so on up to 99. The person with threshold 0 starts. That brings in the person with threshold 1, who brings in the person with threshold 2, and so on until all 100 have joined. Now change one person. The one with threshold 1 now needs two others. The instigator acts alone and nobody follows.

The two crowds are almost identical, and their outcomes are opposite. Collective behaviour depends on the whole distribution of thresholds. The average person's attitude tells you little. The same logic governs who adopts a new tool at work, who speaks up about a problem, and whether a movement starts.

How groups solve it

Elinor Ostrom spent her career studying communities that had managed shared resources for generations: Swiss alpine meadows, Japanese forest villages, Spanish irrigation systems, Philippine irrigation societies. In Valencia, a court of farmers still settles irrigation disputes every Thursday at noon outside the cathedral door. Ostrom showed that communities can avoid the tragedy of the commons without either privatisation or top-down control. In 2009 she became the first woman to win the Nobel Prize in economics. Her design principles for lasting commons, paraphrased:

  1. Clear boundaries: who is in, and what the resource is.
  2. Rules that fit local conditions, with costs in proportion to benefits.
  3. The people affected help make the rules.
  4. Monitoring by people accountable to the users.
  5. Graduated sanctions: small penalties first, larger ones for repeat offences.
  6. Cheap, quick ways to resolve conflicts.
  7. Outside authorities respect the group's right to organise.
  8. For large systems, nested layers of smaller groups.

The biologist Martin Nowak identified five mechanisms that let cooperation evolve (2006). Each works when some quantity is larger than the cost-to-benefit ratio of helping, c/b.

Kin selection

r > c/b

Relatedness exceeds the cost-to-benefit ratio.

Direct reciprocity

w > c/b

The chance of meeting the same person again.

Indirect reciprocity

q > c/b

The chance that others know your reputation.

Network reciprocity

b/c > k

Benefit-to-cost beats the average number of neighbours.

Group selection

b/c > 1 + n/m

Group size n, number of groups m.

The common thread is that cooperation survives when its benefits flow back to cooperators, through shared genes, repeat meetings, reputation, local clusters or competition between groups. That gives a design question for any team or community: how do the benefits of cooperating flow back to the people who cooperate?

A practical toolkit

Shrink it

Split big groups into small units with shared goals. Visibility and reciprocity return at small scale.

Show it

Make contributions and usage visible: shared dashboards, open ledgers, credit by name.

Own it

Give every shared problem a single named owner.

Repeat it

Keep teams and partners stable, and let reputation travel.

Make it pivotal

An all-or-nothing pledge ("I'll pay if 200 others do, and get a refund otherwise") turns a dilemma into a stag hunt, because your money only counts when the goal is reached. Crowdfunding platforms use this design.

Sanction gradually

Start with a reminder. Escalate for repeat offences.

Let the people affected set the rules

Rules people helped write need less enforcement.

Build the norm

Once enough people cooperate, "this is what we do here" enforces itself.

Part 7

Thinking in games

Ten habits of mind carry game theory into daily life.

Name the game before you play it

Most strategic mistakes start with the wrong game: treating a repeated game as a one-off, a coordination problem as a conflict, or a fear problem as a greed problem.

Try this: before a big decision, write down the five building blocks: players, actions, payoffs, information, timing.

Model their payoffs, and discount their words

People respond to the incentives they face, which often differ from the goals they state. Charlie Munger liked to say: show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome.

Try this: when someone's behaviour puzzles you, ask what game would make it sensible.

Look forward, reason backward

Start from where the game ends and work back to today.

Try this: before a negotiation, write down what each side does if there is no deal. For a career move, ask what the decision-makers will look at when the moment comes.

Lengthen the shadow of the future

Most of your games repeat: colleagues, clients, your industry, your city, your family. Reputation compounds. Where the future is short, as with strangers, one-off deals or the last months of a contract, rely on contracts and verification instead of goodwill.

Try this: before a hard move, ask whether you would make it if you had to work with this person for ten more years.

Be nice, retaliate, forgive, stay clear, add a little generosity

Open with cooperation. Respond to defection quickly and in proportion. Return to cooperation as soon as the other side does. Make your rules easy to read. Because the world is noisy, forgive the occasional lapse.

Try this: respond to patterns instead of single incidents.

Diagnose greed versus fear

When cooperation fails, ask whether people are tempted to exploit or afraid of being exploited. Fear needs assurance: go first with something small, offer a guarantee, make commitments conditional. Greed needs structure: monitoring, contracts, aligned incentives.

Try this: before adding a control, check whether the real problem is that people are afraid.

Change the game instead of playing it better

The biggest wins come from changing the players, the payoffs, the rules, the information or the scope. Adam Brandenburger and Barry Nalebuff turned this into a checklist they call PARTS: players, added values, rules, tactics, scope.

Try this: when you are stuck, ask which of the five building blocks you can change.

Make commitments credible, and judge signals by their cost

Words are cheap, so costly actions carry the weight. Put money in, go public, give up an option you would be tempted to use. Judge other people's claims by what it would cost them if they were lying. A threat you would never carry out is no threat.

Try this: ask what it would cost the other side to be wrong.

Use common knowledge to coordinate

Coordination depends on what everyone knows that everyone knows. A series of one-to-one conversations leaves people unsure what the others heard. A public announcement settles it. Michael Chwe showed how rituals and public events do this work in Rational Ritual (2001).

Try this: when you need a group to shift together, say it once, in front of everyone.

Prefer positive-sum games, and play the long ones

Most value in life comes from games where both sides can win: trade, friendship, learning, collaboration. Zero-sum thinking in a positive-sum situation is the most expensive mistake on this list. James Carse divided games into finite ones, played to win, and infinite ones, played to keep the game going. Careers, relationships and reputations are infinite games. Watch for positional races: if everyone doing more leaves everyone at the same rank, agree on limits together or pick a different game.

Try this: ask whether you would still want this if everyone else got it too.

Part 9

Where game theory misleads

People reason only a few steps ahead

In a game where everyone picks a number and the winner is closest to two-thirds of the average, perfectly rational play leads to zero. In Rosemarie Nagel's experiments, first guesses averaged about 36, with clusters at 33 and 22: one or two steps of reasoning (1995). Winning requires modelling how people actually think.

People care about fairness

In the ultimatum game, one player proposes how to split a sum and the other accepts or rejects. A rejection leaves both with nothing. Theory says accept any positive offer. Across 37 studies, proposers offered 40 per cent on average and about 16 per cent of offers were rejected (Hessel Oosterbeek, Randolph Sloof and Gijs van de Kuilen, 2004).

Payoffs are hard to know, including your own

Most of the craft lies in working out what the game really is.

A model is a map

A two-by-two table is a thinking aid. Reality has more players, more options and more noise.

Calculation can corrode trust

Treating people who are on your side as opponents turns cooperative games into competitive ones. In close relationships, the most useful move is often to stop keeping score.
