Game theory, from the axioms up

Game theory is not a theory about games. It is the mathematics of what happens when the best thing for you to do depends on what someone else does — and their best move depends on yours. That circularity is the whole subject. Everything below is built out of it.

This is written in two layers at once: the first-principles layer (what the object is, why each concept has to exist) and the systems layer (what feedback structure each game really is, and where the leverage sits). Read the spine first; drill wherever you want.

00 — Compression

The whole thing in six lines

If you retain nothing else, retain this. Every later section is an unpacking of one of these six.

1. Interdependence. A situation is a game when your payoff depends on other people's choices, not just your own. Otherwise it's a decision problem, and you just optimise.
2. Best response. Given a guess about what others do, one of your options is best. Strategy is the function from their behaviour to yours — not a single move.
3. Equilibrium is a fixed point. A configuration where everyone is already best-responding to everyone else. Nobody regrets their move given the others'. Nothing pushes the system anywhere.
4. Equilibrium ≠ good. The fixed point can be worse for everyone than an outcome they could all have reached. That gap — between what's individually stable and what's collectively best — is where almost all real-world pain lives.
5. Structure beats cleverness. The outcome is mostly determined by four things: who plays, what they can do, what they know, and what they get. Change one of those four and the equilibrium moves. Trying harder inside a bad structure does not.
6. The rules are a choice. The highest-leverage move is almost never playing the game well. It's changing the game — the payoffs, the information, the order of moves, the number of rounds, or who's at the table.
Systems translation. Lines 1–3 say a game is a system of coupled decision rules that settles into an attractor. Line 4 says the attractor need not be desirable — stability and optimality are unrelated properties. Lines 5–6 say the attractor is a property of the structure, so intervention belongs at the structure, not at the behaviour. That is Meadows' leverage-point hierarchy arriving from a completely different direction.

01 — First principles

Building the object from nothing

Start with an empty room and add only what you're forced to add. Each addition below is forced: remove it and you can no longer describe the situation.

Primitive 1: more than one chooser

One chooser facing a fixed world is optimisation. Add a second chooser whose choice affects your outcome and something breaks: you can no longer evaluate your options, because their value isn't defined until you know what the other does. This is the birth defect of the subject, and every solution concept is an attempt to patch it.

Primitive 2: actions, and then strategies

An action is a move. A strategy is a complete contingent plan — what you'd do at every point you might have to act, including points you never expect to reach. The distinction feels pedantic and is load-bearing: threats, promises, deterrence and reputation all live in the parts of the plan that never execute. "I'll match any competitor's price" is a strategy whose value comes entirely from the branch where nobody undercuts you.

Primitive 3: payoffs are preferences, not money

A payoff number is a ranking with an attitude to risk attached (a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility). It already contains everything the player cares about: money, status, spite, fairness, the wish to be seen as tough, next quarter's bonus. When a real person "behaves irrationally", 90% of the time the model just had the wrong payoffs written into it. Payoff misspecification, not human irrationality, is the usual failure.

Practical rule. Before you call someone irrational, write down the payoff matrix under which their behaviour is optimal. Then ask whether that matrix is the real one. It very often is — you were solving a different game than they were playing.

Primitive 4: information

Who knows what, and who knows that others know it. Three separate things get confused here:

  • Perfect information — you can see every move already made. Chess yes, poker no.
  • Complete information — you know the other players' payoffs. Rare in real life; you usually don't know what your competitor is actually optimising for.
  • Common knowledge — I know it, you know it, I know you know it, forever. This is far stronger than "we both know", and coordination collapses without it. It's why announcements, launches and public commitments matter: they don't add information so much as make information common.

Primitive 5: rationality (the weakest version that works)

Rationality here means only: preferences are consistent, and you pick a best response to your beliefs. Not selfish, not calculating, not smart. And you don't even need it — section 10 shows the same equilibria emerging from blind selection with no thinking anywhere in the system. Rationality is a convenient shortcut for "whatever process ends up doing the locally best thing".

The forced construction: best response and fixed point

Now the circularity resolves. Define each player's best-response correspondence: a map from "what everyone else does" to "what I should do". Every player has one. An equilibrium is a profile of strategies that is a fixed point of all these maps simultaneously — plug it in, get it back out.

Nash's contribution was proving such a point always exists in finite games once you allow randomised strategies (via Kakutani's fixed-point theorem). That existence result is what makes the field a science rather than a collection of puzzles: the question "what happens here?" always has at least one answer.

02 — The solution ladder

Every solution concept, and the assumption it buys

Solution concepts look like a grab-bag of names. They're not. They're a strictly ordered ladder: each rung assumes more about the players and, in exchange, predicts more sharply. Knowing which rung you're standing on tells you exactly how much to trust the prediction.

  1. 1Dominant strategyOne option is best no matter what anyone else does. No beliefs about others required at all.Assumes: you can compare your own options. Predicts: strongly. Rare.
  2. 2Iterated dominanceDelete strategies nobody would ever play; that deletion makes new strategies never-playable; repeat.Assumes: common knowledge of rationality. Predicts: sometimes uniquely.
  3. 3Nash equilibriumNobody can gain by unilaterally deviating. The workhorse.Assumes: correct beliefs about others. Predicts: often several answers at once.
  4. 4Mixed NashRandomising is optimal when being predictable is fatal. Always exists in finite games.Assumes: players can randomise, or beliefs are diffuse. Predicts: probabilities, not moves.
  5. 5Subgame perfect equilibriumNash, plus: your plan must be optimal at every point in the tree, including ones you hoped to avoid. Kills threats you'd never carry out.Assumes: sequential rationality. Predicts: which threats are believed.
  6. 6Bayesian NashPlayers don't know each other's types (costs, values, resolve), only a distribution over them. Best-respond to the distribution.Assumes: a shared prior. Predicts: behaviour under uncertainty about who you're facing.
  7. 7Perfect Bayesian / sequentialBeliefs update by Bayes' rule as the game unfolds, and actions are optimal given those updated beliefs. The home of signalling and reputation.Assumes: consistent belief revision. Predicts: what actions reveal.
  8. 8Correlated equilibriumAdd a public signal — a traffic light, a schedule, a norm — that players condition on. Often beats every Nash equilibrium for everyone.Assumes: a shared coordination device. Predicts: how institutions add value.
  9. 9Evolutionarily stable strategyA population mix that resists invasion by mutants. No thinking required — selection does the optimising.Assumes: replication and selection. Predicts: which norms survive.
How to use the ladder in practice. Climb to the lowest rung that resolves your question, and stop. If dominance settles it, you don't need Nash — and a dominance argument is far more robust, because it doesn't require the other side to be smart. Most real strategic errors come from assuming the other side is at rung 5 when they're at rung 1.

The three properties every prediction has

Existence

Is there an answer? Nash: always, in finite games with mixing. This is why the framework is usable at all.

Uniqueness

Usually no. Multiple equilibria are the norm, and the theory alone can't tell you which one you'll land in. History, focal points and norms do that.

Stability

Will the system move toward it if it's off? Nash says nothing about this. Evolutionary dynamics do — and some equilibria are attractors, others are knife-edges.

The multiplicity problem is the most underrated fact in the field. When several equilibria exist, the outcome is decided by something outside the model: precedent, a salient number, a public statement, the way the question was framed. Schelling called these focal points. This is why "let's all meet at the clock tower" works, why round numbers anchor negotiations, and why the first credible standard usually wins a standards war regardless of quality.

03 — The zoo

Six 2×2 games that cover most of life

Almost every two-party strategic situation you'll meet is one of a handful of shapes. The shape is determined entirely by the ordering of four numbers, not their size. Learn the orderings and you can classify a real situation in about thirty seconds.

Notation, for symmetric games: R = reward if both cooperate, T = temptation to defect while they cooperate, S = sucker's payoff, P = punishment if both defect.

GameOrderingEquilibriumThe pathologyReal signature
Prisoner's DilemmaT>R>P>SBoth defect (dominant)The stable outcome is worse for everyone than the available onePrice wars, ad spend, discount escalation, overfishing, doping

The three that actually matter, and how to tell them apart

The whole diagnostic reduces to two questions.

Q1 — If they cooperate, do I want to defect?

Yes → temptation exists (Dilemma or Chicken). No → you're in a Stag Hunt or Harmony, and the problem is trust, not incentives.

Q2 — If they defect, do I want to defect too?

Yes → Prisoner's Dilemma: mutual destruction is the equilibrium. No → Chicken: someone must yield, and the fight is over who.

These need completely different interventions, and confusing them is the most common applied error:

  • Dilemma → change the payoffs. Contracts, penalties, regulation, repeated interaction, side payments. Talk alone does nothing, because talk doesn't move T above R.
  • Stag Hunt → change the beliefs. Here talk works. Announcements, visible early commitments, guarantees, a critical mass of visible adopters. You aren't fixing incentives, you're removing risk.
  • Chicken → change the commitment. Whoever can credibly remove their own option to swerve wins. Rationality is a liability; the visibly irrational, or visibly locked-in, party takes the pot.
The single most useful sentence in applied game theory: "Is this a trust problem or an incentive problem?" A trust problem solved with penalties creates resentment and no cooperation. An incentive problem solved with reassurance creates a very well-attended meeting followed by everyone defecting.

Three experiments worth running

  1. Turn a Dilemma into a Stag Hunt. Load the Prisoner's Dilemma, then lower the temptation payoff (bottom-left blue, top-right red) from 5 to 2. Mutual cooperation becomes an equilibrium. That single number is what a contract penalty, a reputation system, or a repeated relationship actually does — it doesn't appeal to anyone's better nature, it edits T.
  2. Watch a mixed equilibrium refuse to depend on you. Load Matching Pennies and change only the row player's payoffs. The row player's own mixing probability doesn't move; the column player's does. Your randomisation is calibrated to make your opponent indifferent, so it's controlled by their numbers, not yours. This is deeply counterintuitive and completely general — it's why raising penalties for fraud changes how often auditors audit, not how often fraudsters cheat.
  3. Find the equilibrium nobody wants. In Chicken, note there is no symmetric pure equilibrium: both stable outcomes have a loser. The mixed one has a positive chance of the crash. Brinkmanship is not a mistake in the model; it is priced in.

05 — Sequence

Time, threats and the strategic value of losing options

Everything so far assumed simultaneous moves. Let people move in sequence and two new forces appear: order matters, and most threats are lies.

Backward induction

Draw the game as a tree. Start at the end. At each final decision, the player takes their best option — that's not a guess, it's forced. Now that node has a known value, so the decision before it is also forced. Roll backwards to the root. The result is a subgame perfect equilibrium: a plan that's optimal not just overall but at every branch, including branches you're trying to prevent anyone from reaching.

This is the single most transferable technique in the subject. Any time you're planning a negotiation, a launch sequence, a hiring loop or a multi-quarter roadmap: start from the end state and reason backwards about what each party will do when they get there.

Credible vs incredible threats

A threat is credible only if carrying it out is in your interest at the moment you'd have to carry it out. Backward induction is a lie detector for threats.

Commitment: winning by destroying your own options

Here is the deepest inversion in game theory. In one-person decision problems, more options are never worse. In games, having fewer options is often strictly better, because your options are also information the other side uses against you.

The incumbent above wins by making "fight" cheaper or "accommodate" impossible before entry is contemplated: build excess capacity that's worthless unless used aggressively, sign most-favoured-customer clauses that make discounting expensive, publish a price-match guarantee. Cortés burning his ships is the pure case. So is a union's strike fund, a constitutional debt ceiling, an escrow, a public deadline, a resignation-if-this-fails statement.

What makes commitment work

It must be visible, irreversible, and costly to fake. Fail any one and it's just an announcement.

First-mover advantage

Real when moving first commits you and the other must adapt (capacity, standards, land grab). Stackelberg leadership.

Second-mover advantage

Real when information is more valuable than position — you see their move and counter it. Fast-follower strategies, patent-around, category creation.

The test

Ask: does moving first constrain me or inform them? Constrain → move first. Inform → wait.

Systems reading. Commitment is deliberately removing a feedback path. You are cutting the link from "their move" to "my response" so that your behaviour stops being a function of theirs. In systems terms you convert yourself from a responsive element into a fixed constraint — and constraints propagate through the rest of the system as boundary conditions.

06 — Repetition

How cooperation becomes rational

The Prisoner's Dilemma says self-interested parties destroy each other. Reality is full of cooperation between self-interested parties. The resolution is one variable: the shadow of the future.

The master variable, δ

Let δ (delta) be how much you weight the next round relative to this one — a blend of the probability the relationship continues and your patience. Cooperation is sustainable when the one-time gain from defecting is smaller than the discounted stream of losses from the retaliation it triggers.

For a standard Prisoner's Dilemma with payoffs T>R>P>S and a grim-trigger opponent, cooperation holds when

δ ≥ (T − R) / (T − P)

Read the structure, not the formula. The numerator is what you gain by cheating. The denominator is what cheating destroys. Cooperation survives when the future is heavy enough to outweigh the gain. Three levers fall straight out:

  • Raise δ — make the relationship longer, more certain, or more frequent. Long-term contracts, recurring revenue, community membership, repeat games with the same supplier.
  • Lower T — reduce the payoff from cheating. Escrow, milestone payments, caps, audits.
  • Raise the cost of P — make retaliation bite. Reputation systems, public ratings, blacklists.

The folk theorem, and its warning

If players are patient enough, almost any outcome better than the mutual-punishment payoff can be sustained as an equilibrium of the repeated game. That's liberating and useless in equal measure: repetition makes cooperation possible, not predictable. It also makes extortion, cartels and protection rackets possible on exactly the same logic. Repetition is not a moral force. It's an amplifier of whatever the parties can coordinate on.

What Axelrod's tournaments actually showed

Tit-for-tat won not because it was clever but because it had four properties, and these generalise far beyond the tournament:

Nice

Never defects first. Never pays the cost of starting a war.

Retaliatory

Punishes immediately. Exploitation is never profitable against it.

Forgiving

Returns to cooperation the moment they do. Doesn't lock into a death spiral.

Clear

Trivially legible. The other side can learn the rule in one round, which is what lets them adapt to it.

The known weakness: with noise — a defection that was actually a mistake, a late delivery that was a courier failure — pure tit-for-tat enters an echo of mutual recrimination. The fix is generous tit-for-tat: forgive with some probability, or forgive after two offences rather than one. Every functioning human institution does some version of this.

The unravelling problem

If everyone knows the relationship ends at a known round, backward induction destroys cooperation entirely: the last round is a one-shot Dilemma, so defect; knowing that, the second-last is effectively last, so defect; all the way back. A known endpoint kills cooperation. This is why departing employees get locked out, why last-period incentives are perverse, why term-limited politicians behave differently, and why "this is our final round of funding" changes founder behaviour immediately. In practice, uncertainty about the end is what saves us: keep the horizon open and δ stays high.

07 — Information

Asymmetric information: the games about what you can't see

Most real strategic problems are not about what people do. They're about what people are, and can't be verified. This branch is where game theory earns most of its practical money.

The two failures

Adverse selection — hidden type

You can't tell good from bad before the deal. Bad types are attracted by terms priced for the average. The average worsens. Repeat. Akerlof's used-car market unravels this way.

Moral hazard — hidden action

You can't observe effort after the deal. Insured drivers drive worse; salaried agents optimise for what's measured. Contracts must be written on outcomes, which imports risk.

The distinction is before vs after, and the cures differ completely: adverse selection is fixed by screening and signalling; moral hazard is fixed by incentive alignment and monitoring.

Signalling: why the signal must be expensive

A signal only separates types if it is cheaper for the good type than the bad type. That's the entire theory. If the cost is the same for everyone, everybody sends it and it conveys nothing.

  • A degree signals ability partly because it's less painful for the able — Spence's original model works even if education teaches nothing.
  • A long warranty signals reliability because it's cheap for a firm whose products don't break.
  • A peacock's tail signals fitness because it's a genuine survival cost only the fit can carry.
  • A startup founder taking below-market salary signals belief, because the cost is real and it's lower if you expect the equity to pay.
  • A refundable deposit signals intent to show up.
Design rule. Want to know who's serious? Don't ask — ask for something that costs the unserious more than the serious. Every good qualification mechanism, from an application form to a paid pilot, is this rule wearing a costume.

Screening: the same problem from the other side

Signalling is the informed party moving first. Screening is the uninformed party moving first: offer a menu designed so each type self-selects. Insurance deductibles (safe drivers take the high deductible), versioned pricing (heavy users reveal themselves), free tiers with usage caps, interview take-homes. The design condition is incentive compatibility: each type must strictly prefer the option meant for it.

Cheap talk

Costless, unverifiable statements. Cheap talk is informative only where interests are sufficiently aligned. Two people trying to meet can just say where they'll be — nobody gains by lying. A seller saying "this is the best price I can do" carries almost no information, because the incentive to say it is identical regardless of truth. Before believing a statement, ask: would they say this if it weren't true? If yes, it's noise.

Reputation: the bridge between information and repetition

Reputation is what makes an incredible threat credible. A single incumbent facing one entrant can't credibly threaten to fight. An incumbent facing twenty markets in sequence can — fighting the first entrant is an investment in the belief of the other nineteen. Kreps and Wilson showed that a small doubt about your type ("maybe they're the kind who always fights") plus a long horizon is enough to sustain tough behaviour rationally. This is the formal basis for: brands, credit scores, marketplace ratings, diplomatic red lines, and "we don't negotiate with hostage-takers".

The cost side. Reputation is an asset that must be defended, which means you sometimes have to take a locally irrational action to preserve it. A firm that never fights any entrant has cheap peace and no deterrence. A firm that fights everything has deterrence and no profit. The equilibrium is fighting visibly and occasionally, in the cases most legible to observers.

08 — Reverse game theory

Designing the game instead of playing it

Mechanism design flips the question. Instead of "given these rules, what happens?", it asks: given the outcome I want, what rules produce it when everyone acts in their own interest? This is the branch a product manager, regulator or platform operator actually lives in.

The two constraints every mechanism must satisfy

  • Individual rationality (participation): everyone must be better off inside than outside. A perfectly incentive-compatible mechanism nobody joins is worth nothing.
  • Incentive compatibility (truthfulness): acting honestly must be each participant's best move. If gaming pays, you are not running the mechanism you designed — you're running the one people discovered.

The revelation principle says: anything achievable by any mechanism is achievable by one where telling the truth is optimal. That's a huge search-space reduction — you only ever need to look for truthful mechanisms.

Auctions, and the one result worth memorising

FormatHow it worksOptimal bidWhere you see it
First-price sealedHighest bid wins, pays own bidShade below your value; how much depends on rivalsProcurement, construction tenders, most ad exchanges today
Second-price (Vickrey)Highest bid wins, pays second-highestBid your true value — dominantClassic ad auctions, spectrum design, eBay proxy bidding

Revenue equivalence: under independent private values, risk-neutral bidders and symmetric conditions, all four raise the same expected revenue. So the choice between them is never really about revenue — it's about which assumption you're willing to break. Second-price wins on simplicity and truthfulness; first-price wins when bidders are risk-averse or you fear collusion; ascending wins when bidders learn from each other; sealed wins when you want to prevent signalling between bidders.

The winner's curse. When bidders estimate a common value (an oil field, an acquisition target, a hiring decision), the winner is systematically the one who overestimated most. Correct by bidding as if you already know you won — that is, condition on being the highest estimate — which means shading down substantially as the number of bidders grows. Most acquisition overpayment is this, not hubris.

Matching: markets without prices

When money can't clear the market — students to schools, doctors to hospitals, kidneys to patients — you need a matching mechanism. Gale–Shapley deferred acceptance produces a stable matching (no pair who'd both rather have each other), and it's strategy-proof for the side that proposes. Which side proposes decides who gets their better outcomes. Whoever writes the algorithm allocates the surplus.

Voting: the impossibility results

  • Arrow: with three or more options, no ranked voting rule can satisfy a small set of obviously reasonable fairness conditions at once. There is no "correct" voting system, only trade-offs.
  • Gibbard–Satterthwaite: every non-dictatorial voting rule over three or more options is manipulable. Strategic voting isn't a bug to be designed out; it's mathematically unavoidable.
  • Median voter theorem: with single-peaked preferences on one dimension and two competitors, both converge to the median. This explains political convergence, and equally why two competing products drift toward the same feature set.
  • Condorcet cycles: majorities can prefer A to B, B to C and C to A. Which means agenda order determines the winner — whoever controls the sequence of votes controls the outcome, without ever changing a single preference.
The practical version of all three: in any committee, the person who sets the agenda has more power than the people who vote. Design your decision processes with that in mind, and notice when someone else is exercising it on you.

09 — Splitting the surplus

Bargaining and coalitions

Every negotiation has the same skeleton: there is a surplus that exists only if you agree, and a fight over how to divide it. Two questions decide everything.

Question one: what happens if we don't agree?

Your BATNA — the value of no deal — is not a detail, it's the foundation. Bargaining divides only the surplus above both parties' no-deal values. Which yields the most practical negotiation advice in existence: the highest-return work in a negotiation happens before it starts, and consists of improving your outside option or worsening theirs. Arguing well inside the room moves the split a little. A second bidder moves it a lot.

Question two: who is impatient?

In Rubinstein's alternating-offers model, two players trade offers until one accepts, with the pie shrinking each round. The unique equilibrium splits it in proportion to relative patience: the more patient party gets more, and there's no delay at all — the first offer is accepted, because both can compute the ending. Delay in real bargaining is therefore evidence of something the model excludes: private information, internal politics, or theatre for an audience.

Deadlines are weapons

A deadline binding on you alone destroys your position. Quarter-end targets, expiring funding, a flight home — all are impatience made public.

Nash bargaining solution

Maximise the product of gains over BATNAs. Practically: symmetric split of the surplus, weighted by risk tolerance.

Anchoring is real

Formal theory says the first number shouldn't matter. Empirically it does — it acts as a focal point in a game with many equilibria.

Bargaining ranges

Zone of possible agreement = between the two BATNAs. If it's empty, no amount of skill closes the deal, and recognising that early is the win.

More than two: coalitions

With three or more parties, the question becomes who teams up with whom, and what a member is worth. The Shapley value answers it cleanly: your share is your average marginal contribution across every possible order of joining. It's the formal version of "what would break if you left?" — and it's the right lens for splitting equity, allocating credit across teams, or valuing a partner in a consortium.

The core is the set of divisions no subgroup can beat by walking out together. Sometimes the core is empty: every arrangement can be broken by some coalition, so instability is structural and no clever deal fixes it. Recognising an empty core saves months.

Pivotality beats size. In coalition politics, a small party that can complete a majority has power out of proportion to its seats; a large party that no majority needs has almost none. The same is true inside companies: influence tracks being necessary to a winning coalition, not headcount.

10 — No rationality required

Evolutionary game theory

Everything so far assumed reasoning players. Now remove reasoning entirely. Keep only: strategies that vary, payoffs that determine reproduction or imitation, and repetition. The same equilibria reappear.

This is the most important conceptual move in the field for a systems thinker, because it decouples the result from the psychology. Nash equilibrium stops being a claim about how clever people are and becomes a claim about what a selection process settles on. Firms don't need to calculate optimal strategies; markets kill the ones that don't have them. Norms don't need to be designed; the ones that survive transmission are the ones that spread.

ESS: stability against invasion

A strategy is evolutionarily stable if a population playing it can't be invaded by a small group playing something else. It's Nash plus a stability condition — and it's stricter, which is useful: many Nash equilibria are not ESS, and those are the ones that don't survive contact with a real, noisy world.

Hawk–Dove, and why mixed equilibria are populations

Hawks fight, Doves display and yield. Hawks beat Doves. But hawks meeting hawks pay injury costs. So the fraction of hawks self-corrects: too many hawks and fighting costs make dovishness pay; too few and aggression pays. The population settles at a mix determined by the ratio of the contested value to the injury cost.

The reinterpretation matters: a mixed strategy needn't be one player randomising. It can be a stable proportion in a population. "30% of sellers cut corners" is the same mathematical object as "each seller cuts corners 30% of the time" — and the intervention (raise the cost of aggression, lower the value of winning) is the same either way.

Replicator dynamics: the systems view

Strategies that do better than average grow; worse than average shrink. That's it. This turns game theory into a dynamical system with attractors, basins and tipping points — which is exactly the language for the next section.

Attractors

Stable equilibria the system falls into from a whole region of starting points.

Basins

Which starting mixes lead where. In a Stag Hunt, the basin boundary is the trust threshold.

Tipping points

Cross the boundary and the system runs to a different attractor on its own. Adoption cascades, bank runs, norm collapse.

Path dependence

Where you end up depends on where you started. History is a parameter, not noise.

Why this is the practical version. If you want a population to move to a better equilibrium, you have two options: shift the payoffs so the bad attractor disappears, or push the population past the basin boundary hard enough that it runs to the good attractor by itself. The second is much cheaper — it explains why subsidies for early adopters, seeded supply, and launch-day critical mass work, and why the same spend dribbled out over two years does nothing.

11 — The systems layer

Games are feedback structures

Here is the translation that makes game theory compose with everything else you know about systems. A game is a set of coupled decision rules. Iterate them and you get a dynamical system. Every classic game is a recognisable feedback structure, and once you see the structure you already know how it behaves and where to intervene.

Stocks, not just flows

Classical game theory is about flows — decisions, per-round payoffs. The systems upgrade is to notice the stocks that accumulate underneath, because stocks are what create inertia, delay and path dependence:

Trust

Accumulates slowly from consistent cooperation, drains fast from one defection. Asymmetric fill and drain rates are why reputational damage is so hard to reverse.

Reputation / brand

A stock of others' beliefs. Converts incredible threats into credible ones. Spending it is sometimes correct; forgetting it's finite is not.

Commitment capital

Sunk, irreversible positions. Raises your credibility and lowers your flexibility — the same property viewed twice.

Shared resources

Fish, attention, groundwater, user goodwill, buyer patience. Depletable, with a regeneration rate people systematically overestimate.

Leverage points, ranked for games

Meadows ranked intervention points by power. Applied to strategic systems, the ranking comes out almost identically — and almost inverted from where effort usually goes.

LeverageInterventionExample

The observation worth carrying around. Nearly all organisational effort goes into the top row. Nearly all durable change comes from the bottom two. If you're a product manager, the bottom two rows are literally your job description: you decide who is in the market, what they can see, and what the rules of exchange are.

12 — Applications

Where it actually shows up

The point of the classification work is speed: recognise the shape, and you inherit everything known about it.

In games proper

Actual games are the field's laboratory because their rules are unambiguous.

  • Chess, Go — finite, perfect information, zero-sum. Zermelo's theorem says a determinate result exists; it's just uncomputable for us. Strategy here is search under bounded rationality, which is why heuristics and "sense of position" matter more than theory.
  • Poker — imperfect information, and therefore the purest illustration of mixed strategies. You must bluff with some frequency because a never-bluffing player is exploitable, and your bluff frequency is set to make your opponent indifferent to calling. Poker is where the indifference principle stops feeling abstract.
  • Football penalties — Matching Pennies, and the empirical mixing frequencies of professional players are startlingly close to the theoretical prediction.
  • Auctions in games, drafting, tempo — resource commitment under uncertainty, i.e. real auction theory played fast.

In business

SituationGameThe move
Price war between two majorsPrisoner's DilemmaChange the payoff, not the mood: price-match guarantees make undercutting unprofitable — legally, and without any conversation
Entry deterrenceSequential, credibilityCommit before they decide — capacity, exclusivity, a visible cost structure. Threats after entry are worthless

In products and platforms

Product work is mostly mechanism design that isn't labelled as such. You are setting the rules under which strangers with conflicting interests transact, and then living with the equilibrium.

  • Cold start is a Stag Hunt with an empty basin: neither side joins until the other does. The fix is never persuasion; it's subsidising one side past the tipping point, or faking the other side's presence honestly (curated supply, house-run inventory, guaranteed demand).
  • Ratings and reviews are a repetition machine — they convert one-shot transactions into a repeated game against a reputation stock. That single mechanism is what makes trading with strangers possible at all.
  • Discount and coupon escalation between competing platforms is a textbook Dilemma, and it never resolves through restraint. It resolves through differentiation (change the payoff structure) or exhaustion.
  • Ranking algorithms are auctions whether or not money changes hands: sellers spend effort on whatever the ranking rewards. Publish the ranking rule and you get gaming; hide it and you get uncertainty and complaint. Either way, the ranking rule is your product strategy, because it defines the game your supply side is playing all day.
  • Freemium tiers are screening. The free tier must be genuinely useful and genuinely limited in a dimension that correlates with willingness to pay, or it separates nobody.
  • Internal roadmaps are coalition games. A feature ships when it's part of a winning coalition, which is why pivotality — being necessary to somebody's win — predicts influence better than seniority.

In politics

  • Elections — median voter convergence on a single dimension; multi-dimensional issue spaces have no stable centre, which is why agenda-setting and issue framing decide so much.
  • Coalition formation — the Shapley/Banzhaf logic: pivotality, not size. Small pivotal parties extract disproportionate concessions.
  • Deterrence — Chicken with catastrophic payoffs. Mutually assured destruction is a commitment device: it works by removing the option to survive a first strike, and the entire theory of it is Schelling's.
  • Legislative bargaining — Rubinstein with an audience. Public positions are commitment moves that raise the cost of conceding, which is precisely why they're made.
  • Veto players — the more actors whose consent is required, the smaller the set of feasible changes. Institutional design determines policy stability more than the preferences of anyone in office.

In the world

  • Climate — the largest commons problem ever run, with the worst possible delay structure: costs now, benefits later, benefits shared globally. This is why voluntary appeals fail and why the real work is treaty architecture, border adjustments and technology cost curves — payoff editing, not persuasion.
  • Antibiotic resistance and vaccination — commons and free-riding, with the twist that the free-rider strategy is individually rational and stops being so once enough people adopt it.
  • Trade wars — Dilemma with retaliation loops; the WTO's function is to be the repeated-game enforcement mechanism that makes cooperation an equilibrium.
  • Arms control and AI development races — Chicken and Stag Hunt overlapping. Whether it's one or the other depends on whether unilateral restraint is survivable, which is why verification regimes are the whole ballgame: verification converts a Dilemma into a Stag Hunt by removing the fear of being the sucker.
  • Migration, congestion, queues — congestion games, where everyone's best response makes the shared resource worse. Braess's paradox: adding a road can slow everyone down, because equilibrium routing isn't optimal routing.

13 — Limits

Where the theory misleads

A tool you can't criticise isn't a tool, it's an ideology. The failure modes are well mapped.

Failure 1 — the payoffs are wrong

By far the most common. People care about fairness, status, revenge, being seen to be consistent, and not looking foolish in front of their team. The Ultimatum Game is the canonical demonstration: offered a split of £10, standard theory predicts the responder accepts £1, since £1 beats £0. Real responders reject anything under roughly 20–30%, and are willing to pay for the privilege. They're not irrational; spite has positive value in their utility function, and having a reputation for rejecting lowballs pays across future encounters.

Failure 2 — nobody solves to equilibrium

People reason a step or two, not to a fixed point. Level-k thinking models this directly: level-0 plays randomly or naively, level-1 best-responds to level-0, level-2 to level-1. Most people sit at level 1 or 2. This predicts real behaviour in beauty-contest games far better than Nash does — and it has a direct practical corollary: don't out-think the room. Model the depth your counterpart is actually operating at. Assuming your competitor has anticipated your anticipation of their anticipation is usually a modelling error.

Failure 3 — multiple equilibria, no selection rule

The model says "one of these three things happens" and stops. All the interesting work — which one, and why — is done by focal points, history and institutions the model doesn't contain.

Failure 4 — common knowledge is fiction

The theory routinely assumes everyone knows the payoffs, knows everyone knows, ad infinitum. Almost never true. Most real conflicts persist precisely because the parties have different models of the same game.

Failure 5 — the model becomes the belief

Teaching people that defection is rational makes them defect more. Economics students play the Prisoner's Dilemma less cooperatively than others. A model of strategic behaviour is not a neutral description; adopted widely, it edits the payoffs it claims to describe. Treat the framework as a lens for seeing structure, not as permission.

What survives the critique. Almost all of it, if you use the theory the right way round. Its power is diagnostic, not predictive: it tells you what kind of situation you're in and therefore which class of intervention can possibly work. Even a badly-parameterised model that correctly identifies "this is a trust problem, not an incentive problem" beats a perfectly-executed intervention pointed at the wrong one.

14 — Practice

The field guide

Seven questions, in order. Run them on any real situation — a negotiation, a competitor's move, a team dysfunction, a policy — and you'll have a structural read in about ten minutes.

  1. 1Who are the players, really?Include the ones who aren't in the room: the regulator, the other side's board, the internal faction, the future entrant. Getting the player set wrong invalidates everything downstream.
  2. 2What can each actually do?Real options, not stated positions. What can they do that they're not doing? An option they don't have is worth knowing about.
  3. 3What does each get?Write the payoffs from their seat — their incentives, their career, their measurement. Not what you think they should want. This step does most of the work.
  4. 4Who knows what?What's private, what's verifiable, what's merely asserted. Every asymmetry is either a vulnerability or an instrument.
  5. 5Simultaneous or sequential? Once or repeated?This determines whether commitment and reputation are available to you. It's the highest-information question on the list.
  6. 6What shape is it?Dilemma, Stag Hunt, Chicken, coordination, zero-sum, commons. Name it — you now inherit the known interventions.
  7. 7Can I change the game instead of playing it?Ask it last, act on it first. New player, new information, new payoff, new order of moves, new rules. This is where the returns are.

TermIn one line
